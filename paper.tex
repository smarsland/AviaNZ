\begin{abstract}
Automated bird call recognition systems trained on one recording environment often produce worse results when deployed to others. This is due to differences in background noise and recording equipment, a problem known as domain shift. To isolate this issue we process data from two different recording sources into matched datasets with identical species distributions across nine bird species. We also control for the length of both datasets and differences in annotations. We then fine-tune a pre-trained bird recognition CNN on each dataset and find substantial domain shift in both directions, with around a 38--47\% performance loss. We then test different methods for addressing this problem, including normalization of the spectrogram, adding noise, and domain adversarial training. Of the methods we tested we find adaptive background subtraction helps models generalize the most, reducing performance loss to as low as 18--32\%. We find moderate noise augmentation can help, but also hurts performance. Noise diversity shows mixed effects. 
\end{abstract}

\keywords{bird call recognition, domain shift, cross-domain evaluation, bioacoustic monitoring}

\section{Introduction}

The automated recognition of bird species from acoustic recordings has improved markedly in recent years~\cite{stowell2022computational,2018_priyadarshani,2018_stowell}. Deep neural networks processing spectrograms of sound data are able to differentiate between the calls of many species, forming the basis for analysis tools such as AviaNZ~\cite{2019_marsland}, BirdNet~\cite{kahl2021birdnet}, and Perch~\cite{van2025perch}, amongst many others. 

However, models often struggle in test environments due to differences in recording equipment (and configuration settings for recorders) and background noise~\cite{2016_darras}. This problem is called \textit{domain shift} and remains a major problem in bioacoustics, as well as many other areas~\cite{Venkateswara2020}. It is of particular concern for biodiversity monitoring, where many different types of acoustic recorder may be used, and annotated training data may only come from a subset of these machine types~\cite{2018_priyadarshani}.

In this work we examine the extent of domain shift in bird call recognition, and evaluate strategies for mitigating it. To isolate this problem we construct matched datasets with identical label distributions and similar length, but different recording sources. We then systematically compare preprocessing normalization, domain adversarial training, and noise augmentation approaches.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Matched Dataset Construction}

We created two datasets covering nine classes of commonly found New Zealand birds, comprising introduced European passerines (blackbird, \textit{Turdus merula}; chaffinch \textit{Fringilla coelebs}), native and self-introduced passerines (piwakawaka/fantail, \textit{Rhipidura fuliginosa}; riroriro/grey warbler \textit{Gerygone igata}; miromiro/tomtit, \textit{Petroica macrocephala}; silvereye, \textit{Zosterops lateralis}), a parrot (k\=ak\=a, \textit{Nestor meridionalis}), and an owl (ruru/morepork, \textit{Ninox novaeseelandiae}). A final class combined the vocalisations of two oscine species with overlapping dialectic calls (t\=u\=i, \textit{Prosthemadera novaeseelandiae}; korimako/bellbird, \textit{Anthornis melanura}). Individual recordings may contain multiple species; the datasets are matched based on the distribution of species labels rather than exclusive class membership. After matching species across both sources, the DOC dataset contains 766 files (64 minutes total duration) and the Wait\=akere dataset contains 684 files (73 minutes total duration). To ensure identical species distributions for training and evaluation, we downsample both datasets to 674 files each during the train/test split stage. Recordings in both datasets were capped at 10 seconds in length.

The DOC dataset contains bird call recordings collected between 2010 and 2023 as part of the New Zealand Department of Conservation (DOC) baseline biodiversity monitoring. These come from across the public conservation land of Aotearoa New Zealand, and are collected using the DOC AR4\footnote{electronics@doc.govt.nz} recorder sampling at 32 kHz. Bird calls in this dataset were manually identified by a combination of spectrogram visualisation and listening. The species present was recorded per minute of recording, with the calls extracted automatically from these records to make 3-12 second long recordings. The dataset is available at \url{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ollypowell/new-zealand-bird-sound} and has an Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license. We subsequently processed the data to correct some labels and ensure that the labelled species was clearly present. 

Our second dataset comes from recordings from six sites in the Wait\=akere ranges, an area of national park approximately 25 km from the city of Auckland in Aotearoa New Zealand. The data were collected as part of a MSc project~\cite{JoeMsc2024} using a combination of the Frontier Labs Bar- LT\footnote{\url{https://www.frontierlabs.com.au/bar-lt}} and the DOC AR4, both recording at 32 kHz. The data were manually annotated using the AviaNZ software by expert annotators.

For both datasets, spectrograms were extracted in AviaNZ using 25 ms Hann window with a 10 ms hop between windows, and processed into 224 mel-scale frequency bins.

\textbf{Data Splitting:} To prevent data leakage from recording conditions, we employed a two-stage splitting strategy. For the Wait\=akere dataset, which contains multiple annotated segments from the same source recordings, we performed file-level splitting: all segments from a given source file were assigned to either the training or test set, never both. Source files were randomly assigned to achieve an approximately 75/25 train/test ratio, though the actual segment-level ratio varies slightly due to differing numbers of annotations per file. For the DOC dataset we performed sample-level splitting, downsampling to match the species distribution that resulted from the Wait\=akere file-level split, ensuring both datasets have identical total size (674 samples) and comparable class balance across the training and testing sets. This approach balances the need to prevent temporal and acoustic leakage in the Wait\=akere data while maintaining distributional similarity between the two datasets. While the DOC data spans geographically diverse locations, we cannot guarantee full independence between samples due to unknown recording correlations.

\subsection{Normalization Strategies}

We compare six spectrogram normalization approaches to assess their cross-domain robustness: \textbf{Log baseline} is the standard log transform of a linear spectrogram. \textbf{Log+median} applies log transform followed by 5-frame median filter along the time axis. \textbf{Log+normalize} begins with log transform, then estimates background noise using the quietest 10\% of frames per frequency band. Values exceeding 4 standard deviations from this estimate are treated as outliers and replaced by samples drawn from the non-outlier distribution within the same band. We then subtract this background estimate from the original. \textbf{Log+median+normalize} applies the 5-frame median filter before background estimation and subtraction. \textbf{PCEN} is per-channel energy normalization~\cite{Lostanlen2019}. \textbf{Box-Cox} is a standard power transformation for variance stabilization \cite{1964_box_cox}.

\subsection{Domain Adversarial Training}

We evaluate domain adversarial neural networks (DANN)~\cite{ganin2016domain} as a learned approach to domain adaptation. DANN extends standard classification networks with an auxiliary domain classifier trained adversarially via gradient reversal. During training the feature extractor learns representations that are both discriminative for species classification and invariant to domain identity. 

Our implementation adds a domain classification head to the model backbone, taking features from the final layer. The domain classifier consists of three fully connected layers (running from 512 units to 256 to 1) with batch normalization, ReLU activations, and dropout (0.3). This head predicts binary domain labels (DOC vs Wait\=akere). A gradient reversal layer with $\lambda=0.3$ inverts gradients flowing back to the feature extractor, encouraging domain-invariant representations. The domain classifier is trained jointly with the species classifier using binary cross-entropy with logits loss.

\subsection{Noise Augmentation}

To investigate whether synthetic acoustic variation can improve generalization, we augment training spectrograms with background noise sampled from the Freesound dataset\footnote{\url{http://freesound.org}}. During training, noise is applied to spectrograms by mixing: $S_{\text{aug}} = (1-\alpha) \cdot S_{\text{orig}} + \alpha \cdot N_{\text{scaled}}$, where $S_{\text{orig}}$ is the original spectrogram, $N_{\text{scaled}}$ is a randomly selected noise sample RMS-scaled to match the signal energy, and $\alpha = \text{intensity}$ is a fixed mixing ratio.

We systematically vary two augmentation parameters: \textbf{intensity} ($\in \{0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$) controls the maximum noise mixing ratio, and \textbf{variety} (1, 10, 100, or 1000 unique noise samples) controls acoustic diversity. Noise samples are preprocessed identically to bird calls. This design allows us to separate the effects of noise strength from noise diversity~\cite{kumar2024}.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training}

We use a BirdClef 2025-pretrained RegNetY-008 CNN \cite{shionao7_regnrty008_2024} and then apply fine-tuning to our nine species. Training uses the AdamW optimizer (learning rate $10^{-4}$ for backbone, $10^{-3}$ for classifier), batch size 16, cosine annealing, early stopping with patience 15, and mixup with $\alpha=0.25$.

\textbf{Data Augmentation:} During training, we apply standard spectrogram augmentations to improve generalization: time stretching (±10\%, 50\% probability), frequency shifting (±10 mel bins, 50\% probability), temporal rolling (random circular shift along time axis), and SpecAugment-style time/frequency masking (2 rounds per sample, masking up to 20\% of time and 48 frequency bins). These augmentations are disabled during evaluation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Let $D_s$ and $D_t$ denote source (training) and target (test) domains, and let $A(D_s \to D_t)$ denote exact accuracy (all classes) when training on $D_s$ and testing on $D_t$. \textbf{In-domain accuracy} is $A(D_s \to D_s)$, the performance when training and testing on the same domain. \textbf{Cross-domain accuracy} is $A(D_s \to D_t)$ where $D_s \neq D_t$. \textbf{Reduction} is the proportional performance loss relative to the target domain's in-domain baseline:
\begin{equation}
\text{Reduction}(D_s \to D_t) = \frac{A(D_t \to D_t) - A(D_s \to D_t)}{A(D_t \to D_t)}
\end{equation}
This measures how much worse a model trained on $D_s$ performs on $D_t$ compared to a model trained directly on $D_t$, controlling for the intrinsic difficulty of $D_t$. We report both absolute accuracy differences and proportional reduction relative to the target-domain baseline; the latter controls for differences in target-domain difficulty but can become unstable when baseline accuracy is low. Negative reduction values indicate cross-domain performance exceeding the in-domain baseline. This is rare, and typically due to degraded in-domain performance and randomness rather than genuine improvement.

\subsection{Experimental Design}

For every test we train a model on the training data from one dataset and then test it on the test data of both datasets, comparing within-domain to cross-domain accuracy. We test six normalization methods (Log, Log+median, Log+normalize, Log+normalize+median, PCEN, Box-Cox), DANN, and augmentation with Freesound noise at varying intensities (0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8) and diversity (1, 10, 100, 1000 noise samples). All experiments use 5 random seeds (reporting mean$\pm$std).

\subsection{Noise Augmentation}

Noise augmentation shows mixed results (Table~\ref{tab:noise_intensity}). At low intensity (0.2), cross-domain performance remains similar to baseline while in-domain performance shows variability. As noise intensity increases, both in-domain and cross-domain performance generally degrade, with intensity 0.8 showing substantial performance loss. The effect is asymmetric across transfer directions, with DOC→Wait\=akere showing more robustness to moderate noise levels. Noise variety experiments (Table~\ref{tab:noise_variety}) show that increasing acoustic diversity does not consistently improve generalization. At intensity 0.2, variety has minimal impact on Wait\=akere→DOC transfer, while for DOC→Wait\=akere, higher variety (1000 samples) actually increases reduction to 51.7\%, compared to 40.7\% with single-sample noise, suggesting that increasing diversity does not reliably improve generalization and may introduce additional variability.

\subsection{Per-Species Analysis}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Substantial Domain Shift Persists}

Domain shift in bird call recognition remains substantial even with matched species distributions. Using the Log baseline, cross-domain models achieve 30.7\% (Wait\=akere→DOC) and 21.7\% (DOC→Wait\=akere), representing reductions of 46.9\% and 38.2\% respectively from target in-domain baselines. Both transfer directions show substantial performance loss, though neither is dramatically worse than the other. However, aggregate statistics mask significant per-species variation: while some species (fantail, k\=ak\=a, chaffinch) maintain 86--95\% accuracy across domains, others show dramatic degradation (tomtit drops to 69\% in one direction) or asymmetric transfer patterns.

Some preprocessing methods show negative reduction percentages, where cross-domain performance exceeds the target in-domain baseline, but these just seem to be caused by severe drops in performance on the Wait\=akere dataset. 

\subsection{Explicit Normalization Outperforms Learned Adaptation}

Adaptive background subtraction (Log+median+normalize) achieves better cross-domain performance than domain adversarial training. With approximately 700 training samples, DANN provides no major benefit. We hypothesize this occurs because explicit normalization already removes much domain-specific structure—the domain signal DANN would learn to ignore has already been eliminated, leaving insufficient information for adversarial alignment. Learned invariance also typically requires larger datasets. These results suggest that, in low-data regimes, removing domain-specific variation explicitly may be more effective than attempting to learn invariance.

\subsection{Species-Specific Domain Robustness}

Per-species analysis reveals substantial heterogeneity in domain robustness. Species with simple, stereotyped calls such as fantail and k\=ak\=a maintain 90--95\% accuracy across domains in both transfer directions, suggesting their acoustic features are less sensitive to recording conditions. In contrast, species with more complex or variable vocalizations show greater susceptibility to domain shift. Grey warbler accuracy drops from 92\% to 80\% when transferring DOC→Wait\=akere, and silvereye shows similar degradation (95\% to 80\%), suggesting these species' calls may have acoustic properties that interact more strongly with recording equipment or background noise characteristics.

The most striking finding is asymmetric domain shift in tomtit and morepork. Tomtit maintains 93\% accuracy DOC→Wait\=akere but drops to 69\% for Wait\=akere→DOC, a 24 percentage point difference despite using identical preprocessing. This asymmetry is not explained by overall dataset difficulty alone, as other species transfer successfully in the Wait\=akere→DOC direction. We hypothesize this pattern arises from differences in call variability within each dataset or systematic annotation differences. The morepork anomaly (cross-domain accuracy exceeding in-domain baseline in one direction) likely reflects training instability or acoustic confounds rather than genuine cross-domain improvement. These species-specific patterns suggest that domain adaptation strategies may need to be tailored per species rather than applied uniformly across all classes.

\subsection{Dataset Independence and Generalization Gaps}

An unexpected finding concerns the validation-to-test performance gap, which differs substantially between datasets. While early stopping on validation accuracy inevitably creates some optimistic bias in validation metrics for both datasets, the magnitude of this gap differs markedly. For Wait\=akere (file-level split), models achieve 60--62\% validation accuracy but drop to 35--38\% on the test set, a gap of approximately 23--25 percentage points. For DOC (sample-level split), validation accuracy of 70--72\% drops to 55--58\% at test time, a gap of only 14--16 percentage points. 

Since both datasets use identical early stopping procedures, this approximately 10 percentage point difference in generalization gaps suggests the DOC test set may have less independence than expected, or that the data is more homogenous. This finding highlights that geographic diversity does not automatically guarantee acoustic diversity, and raises questions about what constitutes truly independent evaluation data in bioacoustic monitoring.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Our results have limited replication (five repetitions), which constrains statistical robustness. They are also specific to nine New Zealand species (although their calls are relatively representative of many common international species) and have only two recording sources. The two datasets differ in multiple dimensions (equipment, geography, recording practices, potential label noise), preventing causal attribution of observed effects to specific factors; controlled experiments systematically varying individual factors would be needed for stronger causal claims.

A larger study would be needed to test generalization to bigger species sets, other regions, and different equipment. It should also be noted that aggregate accuracy obscures per-class patterns and potential annotation inconsistencies. We infer mechanisms from accuracy patterns without direct feature representation analysis. 

Our study focuses on 9 New Zealand species with approximately 700 total samples, making the dataset fairly small. We also evaluate only one CNN-based architecture, though testing on an Audio Spectrogram Transformer shows similar results so far. Finally, our noise augmentation uses synthetic background noise, where real environmental noise from target domains may provide more effective regularization.
