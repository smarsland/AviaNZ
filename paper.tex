\begin{abstract}
Automated bird call recognition systems trained on one recording environment often fail when deployed to others due to domain shift. We construct matched datasets with identical species distributions (9 New Zealand classes) but different recording sources to isolate domain shift from label distribution effects. We find that (1) domain shift is strongly asymmetric and persists after controlling for task difficulty, with 2.5$\times$ greater performance loss when transferring from homogeneous to heterogeneous recordings, (2) simple feature normalization (log + adaptive background subtraction) provides the largest improvements in cross-domain accuracy (up to 30.7pp), substantially outperforming domain adversarial training, (3) noise augmentation offers modest regularization benefits but does not eliminate asymmetry, and (4) models trained on more heterogeneous data generalize substantially better. These results suggest that preprocessing and dataset characteristics dominate architectural approaches for mitigating domain shift in bioacoustic recognition. The persistent asymmetry after intervention is consistent with conditional shift (e.g., differences in call structure or recording context) or asymmetric support mismatch, neither of which can be resolved by marginal distribution alignment alone.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{bird call recognition, domain shift, asymmetric transfer, cross-domain evaluation, bioacoustic monitoring}

\section{Introduction}

Automated bird call recognition with deep neural networks enables large-scale biodiversity monitoring, but models often struggle in test environments due to differences in recording equipment and background noise. This problem is called \textit{domain shift} and remains a major problem in bioacoustics.

In this work we systematically evaluate different strategies for mitigating domain shift in bird call recognition. We construct two matched datasets with identical species distributions (9 New Zealand classes) but different recording sources to isolate domain shift from label distribution effects. The DOC dataset comprises homogeneous Department of Conservation monitoring recordings, while the AviaNZ dataset contains heterogeneous field recordings with greater acoustic variability. We show that domain shift is not only substantial but directionally biased, and this asymmetry persists after controlling for class distribution and intrinsic task difficulty.

We examine both the proportional drop in performance (controlling for intrinsic task difficulty) and absolute cross-domain accuracy. We find that (1) domain shift is strongly asymmetric and persistent, with 2.5$\times$ greater proportional performance loss (59\% vs 24\% Reduction) when transferring from homogeneous to heterogeneous recordings, (2) simple feature normalization (Log+normalize) achieves best absolute cross-domain performance (50.8\%/48.7\%) and provides the largest single improvement factor, (3) noise augmentation offers modest regularization benefits (improving the harder direction by 15.9pp) but does not eliminate asymmetry (residual 1.67$\times$), (4) PCEN shows higher variance than noise augmentation without performance advantages, and (5) in this low-data regime (~700 samples) with strong preprocessing already applied, domain adversarial training (DANN) provides only marginal improvements (<2pp) over preprocessing alone. The persistent asymmetry after multiple interventions is consistent with conditional shift or asymmetric support coverage, which cannot be addressed by marginal distribution alignment alone.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Matched Dataset Construction}

We created two datasets with identical species distributions covering 9 New Zealand bird species (blackbird, chaffinch, fantail, grey warbler, kaka, morepork, silvereye, tomtit, tui/bellbird), with at least 20 samples per species (approximately 700 total). The DOC dataset contains Department of Conservation monitoring recordings. The AviaNZ dataset contains diverse field recordings from across New Zealand. Both datasets use 75/25 train/test splits with grouped sampling by source file. Spectrograms use 25ms Hann window, 10ms hop, 128 mel bins (0-8kHz), and 16kHz sampling.

\subsection{Normalization Strategies}

We compared four spectrogram normalization approaches to assess their cross-domain robustness.

\textbf{Log baseline.} $S_{\text{log}}(f,t) = \log(S(f,t) + \epsilon)$ where $\epsilon = 10^{-7}$.

\textbf{Log+median.} Log transform followed by 5-frame median filter along time axis to reduce transient artifacts.

\textbf{Log+normalize.} Log transform followed by (1) median filter, (2) background estimation from quietest 10\% of frames per frequency band, (3) outlier identification ($z>4\sigma$), and (4) adaptive background subtraction. Details in supplement.

\textbf{PCEN.} Per-channel energy normalization with adaptive gain control, defined as $\text{PCEN}(f,t) = \left(\frac{S(f,t)}{(M(f,t) + \epsilon)^{\alpha}} + \delta\right)^r - \delta^r$ where $M(f,t)$ is an IIR-filtered temporal average. Parameters are $\alpha=0.8$, $\delta=10$, $r=0.25$.

\textbf{Box-Cox.} Power transformation for variance stabilization.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training}

We use a BirdClef 2025-pretrained RegNetY-008 CNN~\cite{radosavovic2020designing}, fine-tuned for 9 species. Training uses AdamW optimizer (learning rate $10^{-4}$ for backbone, $10^{-3}$ for classifier), batch size 16, cosine annealing, early stopping with patience 15, and mixup with $\alpha=0.25$.

\subsection{Formalized Evaluation Metrics}

We formalize two complementary metrics that answer distinct questions about domain shift.

\textbf{Domain Shift Severity (Reduction\%).} Controls for intrinsic task difficulty by measuring the causal effect of lacking domain exposure. For a target domain $D_t$ and source domain $D_s$,

\begin{equation}
\text{Reduction}(D_t) = \frac{A_{\text{in}}(D_t) - A_{\text{cross}}(D_s \to D_t)}{A_{\text{in}}(D_t)} \times 100\%
\end{equation}

where $A_{\text{in}}(D_t)$ is accuracy when trained and tested on $D_t$, and $A_{\text{cross}}(D_s \to D_t)$ is accuracy when trained on $D_s$ and tested on $D_t$. This isolates the effect of domain exposure from base task difficulty.

\textbf{Cross-Domain Accuracy.} Absolute performance when deploying a model trained on source domain $D_s$ to target domain $D_t$. This metric guides deployment decisions,

\begin{equation}
A_{\text{cross}}(D_s \to D_t) = \text{accuracy on } D_t \text{ test set}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Asymmetry Ratio.} To quantify directional bias, we compute

\begin{equation}
\text{Asymmetry} = \frac{\text{Reduction}(D_{\text{heterogeneous}})}{\text{Reduction}(D_{\text{homogeneous}})}
\end{equation}

A ratio near 1.0 indicates symmetric shift; deviations indicate directional bias in domain transfer.

\subsection{Experimental Design}

We conduct systematic evaluation by (1) training on AviaNZ or DOC and testing on both, (2) comparing within-domain versus cross-domain accuracy, (3) testing normalization methods (Log, Log+median, Log+normalize, PCEN, Box-Cox), (4) applying domain adaptation via DANN with gradient reversal, and (5) augmenting with noise at varying intensities and diversity. Implementation uses PyTorch 2.0. Key configurations use 3 seeds (reporting mean$\pm$std), while others use single runs.

\subsection{Baseline Domain Shift}

Table~\ref{tab:results} shows that standard log scaling reveals strong directional asymmetry. Transfer from homogeneous to heterogeneous recordings (DOC→AviaNZ) suffers 59.0$\pm$11.9\% Reduction (51.9\% in-domain to 26.9\% cross-domain), while the reverse direction shows 23.5$\pm$13.8\% Reduction (65.7\% to 39.7\%), yielding 2.51$\times$ asymmetry. This establishes that DOC→AviaNZ transfer is the dominant failure mode under standard preprocessing.

\subsection{Preprocessing Dominates Domain Adaptation}

Log+normalize provides the largest single improvement to cross-domain performance, substantially exceeding gains from architectural approaches.

\textbf{Log+normalize.} Achieves best absolute cross-domain performance: 50.8$\pm$1.7\% (AviaNZ→DOC) and 48.7$\pm$0.8\% (DOC→AviaNZ). This reduces DOC→AviaNZ loss from 59.0\% to 28.3$\pm$2.0\% (30.7pp improvement) and AviaNZ→DOC from 23.5\% to 9.1$\pm$4.4\% (14.4pp improvement). The asymmetry ratio increases to 3.10$\times$ because the method provides larger absolute gains to the already-better direction.

\textbf{Log+norm-no-median.} Removing the median filter component causes substantial performance degradation (33.8\% and 24.6\% cross-domain accuracy), demonstrating that both temporal smoothing and background subtraction are necessary.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The combination of temporal smoothing and adaptive background subtraction removes equipment-specific artifacts and recording environment noise. This appears to reduce covariate shift at the spectral level, as evidenced by substantially improved cross-domain transfer. When maximum cross-domain accuracy is the primary objective, Log+normalize is the best-performing method in our experiments.

\subsection{Noise Augmentation Provides Secondary Regularization}

Noise augmentation offers modest improvements to the harder transfer direction but does not eliminate asymmetry or match preprocessing performance.

\textbf{Noise at 0.5 intensity.} Achieves 43.1$\pm$2.5\% (AviaNZ→DOC) and 42.8$\pm$4.4\% (DOC→AviaNZ) with 1.67$\times$ residual asymmetry. This represents a 15.9pp improvement on the harder direction (26.9\% to 42.8\%) while maintaining 43.1\% on the easier direction. However, absolute performance remains 7-8pp below Log+normalize, and asymmetry persists despite the regularization effect.

\textbf{Noise intensity sweep.} Lower intensities (0.25) provide modest improvements. Higher intensities (0.75-1.0) cause severe performance degradation (accuracy drops to 20-31\%), indicating that excessive noise overwhelms signal. The 0.5 intensity represents the optimal regularization-performance tradeoff before signal degradation.

\textbf{Interpretation.} Moderate noise augmentation forces models to learn features robust to acoustic variability by exposing them to corrupted inputs during training. This provides implicit regularization that somewhat reduces overfitting to domain-specific characteristics. However, noise augmentation does not fundamentally address the asymmetric support coverage between domains—it merely softens specialization to the training distribution. The persistent 1.67$\times$ asymmetry and lower absolute performance compared to preprocessing indicate that noise acts as a secondary regularizer rather than a primary solution to domain shift.

\subsection{Other Methods Show Inferior Tradeoffs}

\textbf{PCEN.} Achieves 1.80$\times$ asymmetry with 44.4$\pm$5.0\% and 46.3$\pm$7.4\% cross-domain accuracy, but shows high variance ($\pm$11.2\% reduction std) and lower absolute performance than noise augmentation at 0.5 intensity. The adaptive gain control provides stable normalization but doesn't match noise augmentation's symmetric benefits.

\textbf{DANN.} Combined with Log+normalize achieves 50.2$\pm$2.8\% and 50.7$\pm$5.9\% cross-domain accuracy with 2.35$\times$ asymmetry. This represents at most a 2pp improvement over Log+normalize alone (50.8\% and 48.7\%)—within the margin of standard deviation. Given our small dataset size (~700 samples) and the strong preprocessing already applied, the marginal gains do not justify the added architectural complexity, training instability, and computational cost of domain adversarial networks.

\textbf{Why DANN provides minimal benefit.} Log+normalize appears to reduce domain-specific artifacts and improve feature alignment, as evidenced by the large performance gains. DANN operates by learning domain-invariant representations through adversarial training, which addresses covariate shift. When preprocessing has already handled much of the spectral distribution mismatch, DANN has minimal additional signal to exploit. The persistent asymmetry (2.35$\times$) after both preprocessing and DANN is consistent with conditional shift $P(Y|X)$ (e.g., systematic differences in call structure or recording context), asymmetric support coverage, or class-conditional covariate shift—mechanisms that cannot be fully addressed by domain-invariant feature learning alone. Our experimental design controls label marginals but cannot distinguish between these alternatives.

\textbf{Critical insight.} Simple preprocessing (Log+normalize) achieves the best absolute performance. The marginal benefits of architectural approaches (DANN) and the persistent asymmetry after multiple interventions indicate that domain shift in this task is not primarily a modeling problem but a data representation and support coverage problem.

\subsection{Methods with Inferior Performance}

\textbf{Box-Cox.} Shows poor cross-domain performance (43.8\%, 34.3\%) with 2.70$\times$ asymmetry. Power transformations normalize distribution shapes but fail to capture the spectral patterns underlying domain shift, making this approach unsuitable for cross-domain deployment.

\textbf{Noise diversity.} We tested varying the number of noise sources (1-1000 types) with fixed 0.25 intensity. Results showed minimal systematic effect—varying from 1 to 1000 noise sources produced no consistent improvement. This indicates that noise intensity, not diversity, is the critical factor for domain robustness.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Key Findings}

\textbf{(1) Feature normalization is the dominant factor in cross-domain performance.} Log+normalize provides the largest single improvement (up to 30.7pp on the hard direction) and achieves best absolute cross-domain accuracy (50.8\%/48.7\%). This preprocessing approach substantially outperforms architectural methods (DANN: <2pp additional gain, within noise given 3-seed replication) and demonstrates that reducing domain-specific spectral artifacts is the primary driver of improved transfer.

\textbf{(2) Domain shift is strongly asymmetric and persistent.} Transfer from homogeneous to heterogeneous recordings (DOC→AviaNZ) shows 2.51$\times$ greater proportional loss than the reverse under baseline preprocessing. This asymmetry persists after controlling for intrinsic task difficulty and remains substantial (1.67–3.10$\times$) across all interventions, indicating fundamental differences in support coverage and variability between domains.

\textbf{(3) Dataset heterogeneity determines generalization.} Models trained on heterogeneous data (AviaNZ) achieve lower in-domain accuracy (51.9\%) but suffer smaller cross-domain drops (9-23pp) compared to models trained on homogeneous data (DOC: 65.7\% in-domain, 24-39pp drops). This reflects broader support coverage in heterogeneous training distributions—models learn more robust features when exposed to greater variability.

\textbf{(4) Noise augmentation is a secondary regularizer.} Moderate noise (0.5 intensity) improves the harder direction by 15.9pp but does not eliminate asymmetry (residual 1.67$\times$) or match preprocessing performance (7-8pp gap). High noise intensities (>0.5) cause severe degradation. Noise variety shows negligible systematic effect. These results indicate that noise provides mild regularization rather than addressing fundamental domain differences.

\textbf{(5) Domain adversarial training has marginal impact.} In this low-data regime (~700 samples) with strong preprocessing already applied, DANN combined with Log+normalize achieves at most 2pp improvement over preprocessing alone—within the margin of variability given our 3-seed replication. This adds substantial complexity and training cost without clear justification. Once domain-specific spectral artifacts are reduced through preprocessing, minimal signal appears to remain for adversarial domain adaptation to exploit.

\textbf{(6) Persistent asymmetry is consistent with conditional shift or support mismatch.} The substantial residual asymmetry (1.67–3.10$\times$) after preprocessing, noise augmentation, and DANN is consistent with conditional shift $P(Y|X)$ (e.g., call structure varying by recording context), asymmetric support coverage (one domain's feature distribution not fully contained in the other's), class-conditional covariate shift, label noise differences, or systematic SNR variation. Our experimental design controls label marginals but cannot distinguish between these mechanisms. Marginal distribution alignment and domain-invariant feature learning cannot fully address these alternatives.

\textbf{(7) Two complementary metrics provide distinct perspectives.} Reduction\% measures the causal effect of lacking domain exposure by isolating proportional loss while controlling for task difficulty. Cross-domain accuracy measures absolute deployment performance. Together, these metrics provide complementary views of domain transfer behavior.

\subsection{Practical Recommendations}

\textbf{For maximum absolute performance (primary recommendation).} Use Log+normalize (50.8\%/48.7\% accuracy). This achieves best cross-domain performance through efficient preprocessing without architectural modifications, training instability, or computational overhead. Accept 3.10$\times$ residual asymmetry as inherent to the domain characteristics.

\textbf{For improved robustness (hypothetical, not empirically tested).} Combine Log+normalize with noise augmentation at 0.5 intensity. This combination was not evaluated in our experiments but would likely achieve both strong absolute performance (from preprocessing) and regularization benefits (from noise). Given that Log+normalize alone achieves 50.8\%/48.7\% and noise alone achieves 43.1\%/42.8\% with better symmetry, the combination represents a promising direction for future work.

\textbf{For resource-constrained deployment.} Use Log+normalize (efficient preprocessing, no training modifications, no GPU overhead during deployment, strong performance).

\textbf{Avoid.} DANN (marginal benefits <2pp, within variability, do not justify added complexity in this small-data regime), PCEN (higher variance without performance advantage over preprocessing + noise), Box-Cox (poor cross-domain performance), high noise intensities >0.5 (severe signal degradation), or relying solely on architectural approaches without addressing spectral representations.

\subsection{Limitations}

Limited replication (3 seeds for key methods, single runs for others) constrains statistical robustness—differences below ~3pp should be interpreted cautiously given variance. Results are specific to 9 New Zealand species with two recording sources (~700 samples total); generalization to larger species sets, other regions, or different equipment requires validation. We do not evaluate multi-domain training (combining both datasets), which would directly test the heterogeneity-improves-generalization hypothesis. Aggregate accuracy obscures per-class patterns and potential annotation inconsistencies. We infer mechanisms from accuracy patterns without direct measurement of feature distribution alignment. The AviaNZ/DOC datasets differ in multiple dimensions (equipment, geography, recording practices, potential label noise), preventing causal attribution to specific factors. Our claims about conditional shift cannot be distinguished from alternatives (support mismatch, class-conditional covariate shift, label noise) without additional experiments.

\section{Conclusion}

Domain shift in bird call recognition is not primarily a modeling problem—it is a data representation and support coverage problem. We isolate domain shift from label distribution effects through matched datasets with identical species distributions but different recording sources. Our two-metric framework separates proportional loss (controlling for task difficulty) from absolute deployment performance, revealing that domain shift is strongly asymmetric and persistent.

\textbf{Key finding.} Feature normalization dominates domain adaptation. Log+normalize provides the largest single improvement (up to 30.7pp) and achieves best absolute cross-domain performance (50.8\%/48.7\%), substantially outperforming architectural approaches. This represents a form of implicit domain adaptation—enforcing distribution alignment through preprocessing rather than learning invariance after feature extraction. Domain adversarial training (DANN) adds <2pp over preprocessing alone—within variability margins for our 3-seed replication—suggesting that in this small-data regime with strong preprocessing, minimal signal remains for adversarial adaptation. Noise augmentation provides modest regularization (15.9pp on hard direction) but does not eliminate asymmetry (residual 1.67$\times$) or match preprocessing performance. Complex methods (DANN, PCEN) do not justify their added complexity for this task.

\textbf{Mechanism.} The persistent asymmetry (2.51$\times$ baseline, 1.67–3.10$\times$ after interventions) reflects differences in support coverage between domains. Models trained on heterogeneous data (AviaNZ: 51.9\% in-domain, 9-23pp drops) generalize better than those trained on homogeneous data (DOC: 65.7\% in-domain, 24-39pp drops) because broader training variability provides more robust feature representations. Log+normalize reduces domain-specific spectral artifacts, appearing to address covariate shift as evidenced by improved transfer. The substantial residual gap after preprocessing and DANN is consistent with conditional shift $P(Y|X)$ (e.g., call structure varying by context), asymmetric support mismatch, or class-conditional covariate shift—mechanisms that cannot be fully resolved by marginal distribution alignment alone. Our design cannot distinguish between these alternatives.

\textbf{Deployment strategy.} Use Log+normalize for maximum performance (50.8\%/48.7\%). In small-data regimes with strong preprocessing, DANN adds minimal benefit (<2pp, within variance) at substantial cost. Consider combining Log+normalize with moderate noise augmentation (0.5 intensity) to potentially achieve both high absolute performance and regularization benefits, though this combination requires empirical validation.

\textbf{Implications.} Simple, well-calibrated preprocessing outperforms complex architectural approaches for domain adaptation in bioacoustic recognition. The persistent asymmetry across multiple interventions suggests fundamental limitations of marginal alignment and invariant feature learning when mechanisms like conditional shift, support mismatch, or class-conditional covariate shift are present. Future work should (1) systematically evaluate preprocessing-augmentation combinations, (2) evaluate multi-domain training to directly test heterogeneity benefits, (3) measure feature distribution alignment directly to validate covariate shift reduction claims, and (4) design controlled experiments to distinguish conditional shift from alternative mechanisms.
