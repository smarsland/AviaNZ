\begin{abstract}
Automated bird call recognition systems trained on one recording environment often produce worse results when deployed to others due to differences in background noise and recording equipment, a problem known as domain shift. To isolate this issue we process data from two different recording sources into matched datasets with matched marginal label distributions and similar durations. We fine-tune a pre-trained bird recognition CNN on each dataset and find substantial domain shift in both directions, with 18--84\% performance loss depending on preprocessing method. We test different methods for addressing this problem, including normalization of the spectrogram, adding noise, and domain adversarial training. Of the methods tested, adaptive background subtraction helps models generalize the most, reducing performance loss to as low as 18--32\%. Moderate noise augmentation can help at the cost of reduced in-domain performance, while noise diversity shows mixed effects. 
\end{abstract}

\keywords{bird call recognition, domain shift, cross-domain evaluation, bioacoustic monitoring}

\section{Introduction}

The automated recognition of bird species from acoustic recordings has improved markedly in recent years~\cite{stowell2022computational,2018_priyadarshani,2018_stowell}. Deep neural networks processing spectrograms of sound data are able to differentiate between the calls of many species, forming the basis for analysis tools such as AviaNZ~\cite{2019_marsland}, BirdNet~\cite{kahl2021birdnet}, and Perch~\cite{van2025perch}, amongst many others. 

However, models often struggle in test environments due to differences in recording equipment and background noise~\cite{2016_darras}. This problem, called \textit{domain shift}, remains a major challenge in bioacoustics~\cite{Venkateswara2020}, particularly for large-scale biodiversity monitoring where many different recorder types may be used ~\cite{2018_priyadarshani}.

In this paper we examine the extent of domain shift in bird call recognition, and evaluate strategies for mitigating it. To isolate the problem we construct matched datasets with matched marginal label distributions and similar length, but different recording sources. We then systematically compare performance under preprocessing normalization, domain adversarial training, and noise augmentation.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Matched Dataset Construction}

We created two datasets covering nine classes of commonly found New Zealand birds, comprising introduced European passerines (blackbird, \textit{Turdus merula}; chaffinch \textit{Fringilla coelebs}), native and self-introduced passerines (piwakawaka/fantail, \textit{Rhipidura fuliginosa}; riroriro/grey warbler \textit{Gerygone igata}; miromiro/tomtit, \textit{Petroica macrocephala}; silvereye, \textit{Zosterops lateralis}), a parrot (k\=ak\=a, \textit{Nestor meridionalis}), and an owl (ruru/morepork, \textit{Ninox novaeseelandiae}). A final class combines the vocalisations of two oscine species with overlapping dialectic calls (t\=u\=i, \textit{Prosthemadera novaeseelandiae}; korimako/bellbird, \textit{Anthornis melanura}). Individual recordings were capped at 10 seconds in length and may contain multiple species and the datasets are matched based on the distribution of species labels, rather than exact match. After controlling the species distribution the DOC dataset amounts to 766 files (64 minutes total duration) and the Wait\=akere dataset contains 684 files (73 minutes total duration). 

The DOC dataset contains bird call recordings collected between 2010 and 2023 as part of New Zealand Department of Conservation (DOC) baseline biodiversity monitoring across public conservation land in Aotearoa New Zealand, using the DOC AR4\footnote{electronics@doc.govt.nz} recorder sampling at 32 kHz. Bird calls were manually identified by spectrogram visualisation and listening, with species presence recorded per minute and calls extracted automatically to make 3-10 second recordings. The full initial dataset is available at \url{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ollypowell/new-zealand-bird-sound} with Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license. We subsequently processed the data to correct labels and ensure clear species presence. 

Our second dataset comes from six sites in the Wait\=akere ranges, a national park approximately 25 km from Auckland in Aotearoa New Zealand. Data were collected as part of a MSc project~\cite{JoeMsc2024} using Frontier Labs Bar-LT\footnote{\url{https://www.frontierlabs.com.au/bar-lt}} and DOC AR4 recorders, both recording at 32 kHz, and manually annotated using the AviaNZ software by expert annotators.

For both datasets, spectrograms were extracted in AviaNZ using 25 ms Hann window with a 10 ms hop between windows, and processed into 224 mel-scale frequency bins.

\textbf{Data Splitting:} To prevent data leakage from recording conditions, we employed a two-stage splitting strategy. For the Wait\=akere dataset, which contains multiple annotated segments from the same source recordings, we performed file-level splitting: all segments from a given source file were assigned to either training or test set, never both. Source files were randomly assigned to achieve approximately 75/25 train/test ratio, though actual segment-level ratio varies slightly due to differing annotations per file. For the DOC dataset we performed sample-level splitting, downsampling to match the species distribution from Wait\=akere file-level split, ensuring both datasets have identical total size and comparable class balance. This introduces asymmetry between datasets and may partially contribute to directional performance differences. While the DOC data spans geographically diverse locations, we cannot guarantee full independence between samples due to unknown recording correlations.

\subsection{Normalization Strategies}

We compare six spectrogram normalization approaches to assess their cross-domain robustness: \textbf{Log baseline} is the standard log transform of a linear spectrogram. \textbf{Log+median} applies log transform followed by 5-frame median filter along the time axis. \textbf{Log+normalize} begins with the log transform, then estimates background noise using the quietest 10\% of frames per frequency band. Values exceeding 4 standard deviations from this estimate are treated as outliers and replaced by samples drawn from the non-outlier distribution within the same band. We then subtract this background estimate from the original. \textbf{Log+median+normalize} applies the 5-frame median filter before background estimation and subtraction. \textbf{PCEN} is per-channel energy normalization~\cite{Lostanlen2019}. \textbf{Box-Cox} is a standard power transformation for variance stabilization \cite{1964_box_cox}.

\subsection{Domain Adversarial Training}

We evaluate domain adversarial neural networks (DANN)~\cite{ganin2016domain} as a learned approach to domain adaptation. DANN extends standard classification networks with an auxiliary domain classifier trained adversarially via gradient reversal, learning representations discriminative for species classification yet invariant to domain identity.

Our implementation adds a domain classification head to the model backbone, taking features from the final layer. The domain classifier consists of three fully connected layers (running from 512 units to 256 to 1) with batch normalization, ReLU activations, and dropout (0.3). This head predicts binary domain labels (DOC vs Wait\=akere). A gradient reversal layer with $\lambda=0.3$ inverts gradients flowing back to the feature extractor, encouraging domain-invariant representations. The domain classifier is trained jointly with the species classifier using binary cross-entropy with logits loss.

\subsection{Noise Augmentation}

To investigate whether synthetic acoustic variation can improve generalization, we augment training spectrograms with background noise sampled from the Freesound dataset\footnote{\url{http://freesound.org}}. During training, noise is applied to spectrograms by mixing: $S_{\text{aug}} = (1-\alpha) \cdot S_{\text{orig}} + \alpha \cdot N_{\text{scaled}}$, where $S_{\text{orig}}$ is the original spectrogram, $N_{\text{scaled}}$ is a randomly selected noise sample RMS-scaled to match the signal energy, and $\alpha$ is a fixed mixing ratio.

We systematically vary two augmentation parameters: \textbf{intensity} ($\in \{0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$) controls the noise mixing ratio, and \textbf{variety} (1, 10, 100, or 1000 unique noise samples) controls acoustic diversity. Noise samples are preprocessed identically to bird calls. This design allows us to separate the effects of noise strength from noise diversity~\cite{kumar2024}.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training}

We use a BirdClef 2025-pretrained RegNetY-008 CNN \cite{shionao7_regnrty008_2024} fine-tuned to our nine classes. Training uses the AdamW optimizer (learning rate $10^{-4}$ for backbone, $10^{-3}$ for classifier), batch size 16, cosine annealing, early stopping with patience 15, and mixup with $\alpha=0.25$.

\textbf{Data Augmentation:} During training, we apply standard spectrogram augmentations to improve generalization: time stretching (±10\%, 50\% probability), frequency shifting (±10 mel bins, 50\% probability), temporal rolling (random circular shift along time axis), and SpecAugment-style time/frequency masking (2 rounds per sample, masking up to 20\% of time and 48 frequency bins). These augmentations are disabled during evaluation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Let $D_s$ and $D_t$ denote source (training) and target (test) domains, and let $A(D_s \to D_t)$ denote exact accuracy (all classes) when training on $D_s$ and testing on $D_t$. \textbf{In-domain accuracy} is $A(D_s \to D_s)$, the performance when training and testing on the same domain. \textbf{Cross-domain accuracy} is $A(D_s \to D_t)$ where $D_s \neq D_t$. \textbf{Reduction} is the proportional performance loss relative to the target domain's in-domain baseline:
\begin{equation}
\text{Reduction}(D_s \to D_t) = \frac{A(D_t \to D_t) - A(D_s \to D_t)}{A(D_t \to D_t)}
\end{equation}
This measures how much worse a model trained on $D_s$ performs on $D_t$ compared to a model trained directly on $D_t$, controlling for intrinsic difficulty of $D_t$. We report both absolute accuracy differences and proportional reduction relative to target-domain baseline; the latter controls for differences in target-domain difficulty but can become unstable when baseline accuracy is low. Negative reduction values indicate cross-domain performance exceeding in-domain baseline, typically due to degraded in-domain performance rather than genuine improvement.

\subsection{Experimental Design}

For every test we train a model on the training data from one dataset and then test it on the test data of both datasets, comparing within-domain to cross-domain accuracy. We test the six normalization methods (Log, Log+median, Log+normalize, Log+median+normalize, PCEN, Box-Cox), DANN, and augmentation with Freesound noise at varying intensities (0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8) and diversity (1, 10, 100, 1000 noise samples). All experiments use 5 random seeds (reporting mean$\pm$std).

\section{Results}

We evaluate models using exact-match accuracy: a prediction is correct only if all species labels match the ground truth. This is a harsh metric for multi-label evaluation, particularly when most clips contain a single species.

\subsection{Normalization Methods}

Log+median+normalize achieves the best cross-domain accuracy in both directions (Table~\ref{tab:results}), retaining 68\% of the DOC baseline and 82\% of the Wait\=akere baseline. All methods show substantial domain shift, with reduction percentages ranging from 18--84\% depending on the method and transfer direction. Some preprocessing methods (e.g., Log+median) catastrophically degrade in-domain performance, producing misleadingly large negative reduction values that indicate method failure rather than genuine cross-domain improvement. PCEN occasionally improves cross-domain performance beyond in-domain baselines (Wait\=akere→DOC: 32.5\% cross vs 29.1\% in-domain), though this likely reflects inconsistent in-domain optimization rather than true generalization gains.

\subsection{Noise Augmentation}

Noise augmentation shows limited and inconsistent benefits (Figure~\ref{fig:noise}). Moderate noise intensity (around 0.2) maintains cross-domain performance while slightly improving in-domain accuracy on Wait\=akere, though higher intensities (0.6--0.8) degrade it. Noise variety shows little clear effect as well.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Substantial Domain Shift}

Domain shift in bird call recognition remains substantial even with matched species distributions. Using the log baseline, cross-domain models achieve only 30.7\% (Wait\=akere→DOC) and 21.7\% (DOC→Wait\=akere), representing 46.9\% and 38.2\% reductions from target in-domain baselines.

\subsection{Explicit Normalization Outperforms Learned Adaptation}

Adaptive background subtraction (Log+median+normalize) achieves better cross-domain performance than domain adversarial training. With approximately 700 training samples, DANN provides little to no benefit. We hypothesize this occurs because explicit normalization already removes domain-specific structure, leaving insufficient signal for adversarial alignment. In low-data regimes, preprocessing targeting known variation sources (background noise, recording characteristics) may be preferable to learned adaptation methods, which may require larger samples to disentangle domain from task-relevant features.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Our results have limited replication (five repetitions) and cover only nine species present in New Zealand  with two recording sources and approximately 700 samples total. The datasets differ in multiple dimensions (equipment, geography, recording practices), preventing causal attribution to specific factors. We evaluate only one CNN-based architecture, though testing on an Audio Spectrogram Transformer~\cite{ast} shows similar results. Our noise augmentation uses synthetic background noise; real environmental noise from target domains may provide more effective regularization. Aggregate accuracy obscures per-class patterns and potential annotation inconsistencies, and we infer mechanisms from accuracy patterns without direct feature analysis.
