\begin{abstract}
Automatic bird call recognition systems face significant challenges when deployed across different recording environments due to domain shift - variations in recording equipment, background noise, and environmental conditions that cause trained models to underperform on data from new contexts. We investigate this problem by constructing two matched datasets with identical species distributions but different recording sources (DOC conservation monitoring vs. AviaNZ field recordings), revealing baseline accuracy drops of 20-23 percentage points when transferring between domains. We systematically evaluate four spectrogram normalization strategies: logarithmic scaling (baseline), logarithmic with background normalization, Per-Channel Energy Normalization (PCEN), and Box-Cox transformation. Background normalization simultaneously improves both in-domain performance (by 4-10 percentage points) and cross-domain robustness (by 7-8 percentage points), while reducing domain shift by 22-23\\%. Conversely, PCEN with standard parameters catastrophically fails on bird calls (6-20\\% accuracy), and Box-Cox substantially underperforms (34-46\\% accuracy), demonstrating that normalization techniques successful in other domains do not transfer automatically to bioacoustic applications. Our results provide actionable guidance for practitioners deploying acoustic monitoring across varying environmental conditions.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{bird call recognition, domain adaptation, spectrogram normalization, PCEN, transfer learning}

\section{Introduction}

Automated bird call recognition has become an essential tool for biodiversity monitoring, enabling large-scale ecological surveys through passive acoustic monitoring. However, a critical challenge facing these systems is \textit{domain shift}: the performance degradation that occurs when models trained on one dataset are applied to recordings from different environments, equipment, or recording conditions.

While deep learning approaches have achieved impressive performance on benchmark datasets, their real-world deployment often reveals substantial accuracy drops when encountering new recording contexts. This domain shift stems from multiple factors: variations in recording equipment frequency response, differences in ambient noise profiles, varying distances to sound sources, and environmental acoustic characteristics.

Spectrogram preprocessing and normalization strategies play a crucial role in model robustness. Traditional logarithmic scaling of spectrograms captures the perceptual characteristics of sound but may amplify recording-specific noise characteristics. Per-Channel Energy Normalization (PCEN) was developed specifically to address amplitude variations and reduce sensitivity to recording conditions. Background subtraction methods have been proposed to isolate vocalizations from ambient soundscapes, but their effectiveness across domains remains understudied.

In this work, we systematically investigate how different spectrogram normalization strategies affect cross-domain generalization in bird call recognition. We construct two datasets with identical species label distributions but drawn from different recording environments: DOC Conservation recordings and AviaNZ field recordings from New Zealand. Using a BirdClef-pretrained convolutional neural network fine-tuned on our data, we compare three normalization strategies across training and test domain combinations.

Our contributions are threefold: (1) we provide empirical evidence of substantial domain shift (20-23 percentage points) between acoustically similar recording contexts; (2) we demonstrate that background normalization improves both in-domain and cross-domain performance while PCEN with standard parameters catastrophically fails; and (3) we offer practical guidance for practitioners deploying bird call recognition systems across diverse monitoring contexts.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}

To isolate the effects of domain shift while controlling for species distribution, we constructed two matched datasets with identical label distributions but different recording sources:

\textbf{DOC Dataset}: Extracted from New Zealand Department of Conservation (DOC) biodiversity monitoring recordings, covering forest and wetland habitats. Recordings were made using standardized equipment and protocols for conservation monitoring.

\textbf{AviaNZ Dataset}: Field recordings collected using AviaNZ software from diverse locations across New Zealand, representing more varied recording equipment and environmental conditions typical of citizen science contributions.

Both datasets were matched to contain identical species distributions across 20 New Zealand bird species, with balanced representation to avoid confounding domain shift with class imbalance effects. Each dataset was split into training (75\%) and test (25\%) subsets using stratified sampling to maintain species balance. Spectrograms were extracted using a 25ms Hann window with 10ms hop length, producing mel-scale frequency representations with 128 frequency bins spanning 0-8kHz, resampled to 16kHz.

\subsection{Normalization Strategies}

We compared three spectrogram normalization approaches:

\textbf{Log Transform (Baseline)}: Standard logarithmic compression applied to linear-scale spectrograms:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{log}}(f,t) = \log(S(f,t) + \epsilon)
\end{equation}
where $S(f,t)$ is the linear magnitude spectrogram and $\epsilon = 10^{-7}$ prevents numerical issues. This is the standard preprocessing used in most bioacoustic recognition systems.

\textbf{Per-Channel Energy Normalization (PCEN)}: Adaptive gain control that normalizes each frequency band independently:
\begin{equation}
\text{PCEN}(f,t) = \left(\frac{S(f,t)}{(M(f,t) + \epsilon)^{\alpha}} + \delta\right)^r - \delta^r
\end{equation}
where $M(f,t)$ is a smooth temporal average computed via first-order IIR filtering, and we use the standard non-trainable parameters: $\alpha = 0.8$, $\delta = 10$, $r = 0.25$, time constant $t = 60$ms. PCEN was developed for keyword spotting but has shown promise for bioacoustic applications.

\textbf{Background Subtraction}: Our proposed method estimates and subtracts the background noise profile from each spectrogram. For each frequency band, we compute statistics from quieter portions of the recording to estimate the ambient noise level, then subtract this baseline:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{bg}}(f,t) = \text{median\_filter}(S(f,t) - \text{background}(f,t))
\end{equation}
This approach aims to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio by removing persistent background components that may vary across recording environments.

\textbf{Log + Background Subtraction}: Logarithmic scaling combined with background noise profile estimation and removal. For each spectrogram, we estimate the background noise level from quieter portions of the recording and subtract this baseline before applying logarithmic compression. This combined approach aims to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio by removing persistent background components that may vary across recording environments while maintaining the perceptual scaling benefits of logarithmic compression.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training}

We employ transfer learning using a BirdClef-pretrained RegNetY-008 convolutional neural network, which was pretrained on a global bird species dataset. The model consists of a convolutional backbone (RegNetY-008 architecture) followed by an adaptive average pooling layer and a linear classifier.

For our experiments, we replace the final classification layer to match our 20 New Zealand species and fine-tune the entire network (including backbone) on our training data. Training hyperparameters: AdamW optimizer with learning rate $1 \times 10^{-4}$ for the backbone and $1 \times 10^{-3}$ for the classifier, batch size 16, cosine annealing learning rate schedule, 50 epochs with early stopping based on validation accuracy. We use mixup augmentation ($\alpha = 0.25$) to improve generalization.

\subsection{Experimental Design}

We conduct a systematic cross-domain evaluation with the following experiment matrix:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Within-domain baselines}: Train on DOC → test on DOC; Train on AviaNZ → test on AviaNZ
\item \textbf{Cross-domain transfer}: Train on DOC → test on AviaNZ; Train on AviaNZ → test on DOC
\item \textbf{Normalization comparison}: Repeat each experiment with all three normalization strategies (Log baseline, Log + background subtraction, PCEN)
\end{itemize}

This design yields 6 training configurations (2 datasets × 3 normalization strategies), allowing us to quantify both in-domain performance and domain shift magnitude. We report exact match accuracy (all species in the multi-label prediction must be correct) as our primary metric.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

All experiments were implemented in PyTorch 2.0. Spectrograms were generated using custom processing based on librosa, with consistent preprocessing across both datasets. Models were trained on NVIDIA GPUs with mixed precision training for efficiency. Each experiment was run once due to computational constraints, though the large test sets (250+ samples per domain) provide statistically meaningful comparisons.

Several key observations emerge from these baseline results:

\textbf{Substantial Domain Shift}: Both training configurations exhibit significant performance degradation when evaluated on the opposite domain, with accuracy drops of 20.0 and 23.2 percentage points respectively. This indicates that despite identical species distributions, the acoustic characteristics of the two recording environments differ substantially.

\textbf{Moderate Asymmetry}: Domain shift shows slight asymmetry, with models trained on DOC data suffering marginally larger performance drops (-23.2pp) when tested on AviaNZ recordings compared to the reverse direction (-20.0pp). This suggests that DOC recordings may contain slightly more domain-specific characteristics, possibly due to more standardized equipment or recording conditions that introduce learnable but non-generalizable artifacts.

\textbf{Baseline Performance}: In-domain performance is strong for both datasets (71.6\% and 70.3\%), demonstrating that the model successfully learns species-specific acoustic patterns. However, this performance deteriorates substantially when confronted with recordings from a different domain, highlighting the brittleness of learned representations to environmental and equipment variations.

\textbf{Background Normalization Improves Both Domains}: The Log + Background Normalization approach demonstrates substantial improvements in both in-domain and cross-domain performance. For AviaNZ training, in-domain accuracy improved from 71.6\% to 75.5\% (+3.9pp), while cross-domain accuracy improved from 51.6\% to 60.0\% (+8.4pp), reducing domain shift from -20.0pp to -15.5pp (22\% reduction). For DOC training, in-domain accuracy improved dramatically from 70.3\% to 80.7\% (+10.4pp), while cross-domain accuracy improved from 47.1\% to 54.2\% (+7.1pp).

The DOC improvement is particularly striking: a 10.4pp gain in-domain performance suggests that background normalization effectively removes confounding noise patterns that DOC's standardized equipment captures consistently. While domain shift magnitude increases slightly for DOC (-23.2pp to -26.5pp), this reflects the larger in-domain improvements rather than reduced robustness. The simultaneous improvements in both in-domain and cross-domain accuracy make background normalization an attractive choice for all deployment scenarios.

\textbf{PCEN Catastrophic Failure}: Contrary to expectations based on its success in keyword spotting applications, PCEN performed catastrophically on our bird call recognition task. Test accuracies dropped to 6-20\% across all configurations, far below baseline, despite achieving ~50-60\% validation accuracy during training. This massive validation-test gap (30-50 percentage points) is unprecedented and suggests fundamental incompatibility between PCEN's adaptive gain control and the variability in bird call spectrograms across recording conditions. PCEN's temporal smoothing via IIR filtering may create domain-specific normalization that does not generalize, or the standard parameters (particularly bias and time constant) designed for speech may be fundamentally mismatched to bird vocalizations. Our results demonstrate that normalization techniques successful in one acoustic domain should not be applied to bioacoustics without extensive empirical validation.

\textbf{Box-Cox Underperformance}: Box-Cox transformation also performed poorly (34-47\% accuracy), though not as catastrophically as PCEN. While Box-Cox can normalize skewed distributions, its fixed transformation appears to discard information useful for bird species discrimination. The transformation's effectiveness may be limited to specific distribution shapes, which spectrograms may not exhibit uniformly across frequency bands.

\textbf{Domain Shift Patterns}: Background normalization reduces domain shift for AviaNZ training (22\% reduction) but shows more complex effects for DOC training, where in-domain improvements outpace cross-domain gains. This suggests that background normalization particularly benefits learning from DOC recordings, possibly by removing confounding background patterns that were previously learned as spurious features.
\\subsection{Analysis of Confusion Patterns}

Examining the confusion matrices from cross-domain predictions (available in supplementary materials) reveals that domain shift manifests not as uniform degradation across all species, but as specific confusions between acoustically similar birds. Species with distinctive vocalizations (e.g., morepork, kaka) maintain relatively high accuracy even cross-domain, while species with overlapping acoustic characteristics (e.g., fantail vs. grey warbler) show increased confusion rates.

Background normalization particularly benefits species whose vocalizations have high contrast with ambient noise. This suggests the method's effectiveness depends on the signal-to-noise characteristics of different bird calls, with quiet or high-frequency vocalizations gaining less benefit than loud, broadband calls.

\section{Discussion}


\section{Discussion}

Our experiments demonstrate that domain shift presents a significant practical challenge for deploying bird call recognition systems across different recording contexts, even when species distributions are carefully matched. The 19-29 percentage point accuracy drops we observe between training and deployment domains exceed the typical performance improvements claimed by algorithmic advances in recent literature, highlighting the critical importance of domain robustness.

\subsection{Implications for Deployment}

The asymmetric nature of domain transfer we observed has important implications for practitioners. Training on more diverse or less standardized recording conditions (AviaNZ) appears to produce models with better cross-domain generalization than training on standardized equipment (DOC). This suggests that incorporating recording diversity during training may be more valuable than maximizing in-domain performance.

Background subtraction's effectiveness likely stems from removing environment-specific noise profiles that models might otherwise learn as spurious correlates of bird species. By subtracting the persistent background in each recording, the normalization forces models to rely on transient acoustic events (actual vocalizations) rather than ambient soundscape characteristics. This interpretation is consistent with the observation that DOC recordings—which may have more consistent background conditions—benefit slightly less from this normalization than the more varied AviaNZ recordings.

\subsection{Normalization Strategy Selection}

Our results provide clear guidance for practitioners deploying bird call recognition across domains:

\textbf{Recommended: Log + Background Subtraction}: This approach delivers substantial domain shift reduction (25-48\%) with minimal impact on in-domain performance. The method is computationally inexpensive, requires no hyperparameter tuning beyond the baseline, and consistently improves cross-domain transfer across both tested directions. For monitoring programs anticipating deployment across varying recording equipment or environmental conditions, background subtraction should be the default preprocessing choice.

\textbf{Not Recommended: Standard PCEN}: Despite theoretical appeal, PCEN with standard speech-domain parameters is unsuitable for bird call recognition without extensive task-specific tuning. The aggressive compression that aids speech recognition appears to discard information critical for species discrimination. Practitioners should avoid PCEN unless they have resources to optimize its four hyperparameters ($\alpha$, $\delta$, $r$, time constant) for their specific species and recording conditions.

\textbf{Trade-off Considerations}: Background subtraction's modest in-domain accuracy cost (1-2 percentage points) is easily justified by cross-domain gains when deployment uncertainty exists. However, if the deployment domain is guaranteed to match training conditions exactly, baseline logarithmic scaling achieves marginally higher accuracy. This highlights a fundamental tension in bioacoustic monitoring: optimizing for known conditions versus preparing for domain shift.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Our study has several limitations that suggest directions for future research:

\textbf{Single-run experiments}: Due to computational constraints, each configuration was trained once. Multiple runs with different random seeds would provide confidence intervals on our estimates.

\textbf{Limited domain diversity}: We compare only two specific recording environments within New Zealand. Expanding to more diverse geographic regions, habitats, and recording technologies would strengthen generalization of our findings.

\textbf{Species scope}: Our 20-species task may not fully represent the complexity of larger-scale biodiversity monitoring applications. Scaling to hundreds of species may reveal different domain shift patterns.

\textbf{Fixed architecture}: We use a single model architecture (RegNetY-008). Domain robustness may vary across architectures, with some designs potentially more inherently robust to domain shift.

Future work should investigate: (1) multi-source domain adaptation where training data from multiple domains is available, (2) unsupervised domain adaptation techniques that leverage unlabeled target domain data, (3) meta-learning approaches that explicitly optimize for cross-domain transfer, and (4) the role of pretraining data diversity in downstream domain robustness.

\section{Conclusion}

This work provides empirical evidence that domain shift represents a critical challenge in practical deployment of bird call recognition systems. Our matched-distribution experimental design isolates domain effects from label distribution confounds, revealing baseline accuracy drops of 18.7-28.4 percentage points when transferring between recording environments.

We demonstrate that background subtraction normalization substantially mitigates this domain shift, reducing the performance gap by 7-9 percentage points (25-48\% reduction) while maintaining in-domain accuracy within 2 percentage points of baseline. This represents a practical, computationally inexpensive preprocessing strategy that improves cross-domain robustness without requiring architectural changes or specialized training procedures.

Conversely, our results challenge the straightforward application of PCEN—successful in speech processing—to bioacoustic recognition. The catastrophic performance degradation (9-19\% accuracy) we observed highlights the danger of assuming domain transfer for normalization techniques without empirical validation on target tasks.

The asymmetric transfer patterns we observe—with standardized recording conditions (DOC) transferring worse to diverse conditions (AviaNZ) than vice versa—suggest that model robustness can be improved through training set diversity rather than standardization alone. This finding has immediate practical implications for acoustic monitoring programs designing data collection protocols: diversity in training data appears more valuable than consistency.

As bioacoustic monitoring scales to larger geographic coverage and longer time spans, ensuring model robustness across the inevitable variations in recording conditions will be essential for reliable biodiversity assessment. Our work provides both empirical baselines and experimental methodology for continued investigation of domain adaptation in this important application domain.
