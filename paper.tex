\begin{abstract}
Automated bird call recognition systems trained on one recording environment often fail when deployed to others due to differences in background noise and recording equipment. To understand how to deal with this issue we construct matched datasets with identical species distributions but distinct recording characteristics, and evaluate cross-domain generalization. We find that domain shift is strongly asymmetric and models which have been trained on homogeneous recordings suffer greater performance loss when transferred to heterogeneous environments than the reverse. Simple preprocessing by log scaling with adaptive background subtraction yields the largest improvements in cross-domain accuracy, slightly outperforming PCEN, Box-Cox transforms, and domain adversarial training on its own. Noise augmentation provides modest regularization but does not reduce this asymmetry. Surprisingly the variation in noise doesn't seem particularly important. These results indicate that explicit normalization is more effective than learned adaptation strategies in low-data bioacoustic settings, and that pre-trained models remain sensitive to recording conditions. 
\end{abstract}

\keywords{bird call recognition, domain shift, asymmetric transfer, cross-domain evaluation, bioacoustic monitoring}

\section{Introduction}

The automated recognition of bird species from acoustic recordings has improved markedly in recent years~\cite{stowell2022computational,2018_priyadarshani}. Deep neural networks processing spectrograms of sound data are able to differentiate between the calls of many species, forming the basis for analysis tools such as AviaNZ~\cite{2019_marsland}, BirdNet~\cite{kahl2021birdnet}, and Perch~\cite{van2025perch}, amongst many others. 

However, models often struggle in test environments due to differences in recording equipment (and configuration settings for recorders) and background noise. This problem is called \textit{domain shift} and remains a major problem in bioacoustics, as well as many other areas~\cite{}. It is of particular concern for biodiversity monitoring, where many different types of acoustic recorder may be used, and annotated training data may only come from a subset of these machine types~\cite{2018_priyadarshani}.

In this work we demonstrate the problem of domain shift and examine the effectiveness of different strategies for mitigating it in bird recognition. \section{Methods}

\subsection{Matched Dataset Construction}

We created two datasets with identical species distributions covering nine classes of commonly found New Zealand birds, comprising introduced European passerines (blackbird, chaffinch), native and self-introduced passerines (piwakawaka/fantail, riroriro/grey warbler, miromiro/tomtit, silvereye) a parrot (kaka) and owl (ruru/morepork). A final class combined the vocalisations of two oscine species with overlapping dialectic calls (t\=u\=i and korimako/bellbird). In each dataset there were at least 20 samples per class (approximately 700 total). \todo[inline]{Use binomial names?}

The DOC dataset contains of passive acoustic monitoring data collected between 2010 and 2023 as part of the Department of Conservation (DOC) baseline biodiversity monitoring. These come from across the public conservation land of Aotearoa New Zealand, and are collected using the DOC AR4\footnote{\url{electronics@doc.govt.nz}} recorder sampling at 32 kHz. Bird calls in this dataset were manually identified by a combination of spectrogram visualisation and listening to record the species present within one minute of recording, with the calls extracted automatically from these records to make 3-12 second long recordings. The dataset is available at \url{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ollypowell/new-zealand-bird-sound} and has an Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license. We subsequently processed the data to correct some labels and ensure that the labelled species was clearly present. 

Our second dataset comes from six sites in the Wait\=akere ranges, an area of national park approximately 25 km from the city of Auckland in Aotearoa New Zealand. The data were collected as part of a MSc project~\cite{JoeMsc2024} using a combination of the Frontier Labs Bar-LT\footnote{\url{https://www.frontierlabs.com.au/bar-lt}} and the DOC AR4, both recording at 32 kHz. The data were manually annotated using the AviaNZ software by expert annotators. \todo[inline]{**** How long are the recordings? }

For both datasets, all audio was resampled to 32 kHz. Spectrograms were then extracted in AviaNZ using 25 ms Hann window with a 10 ms hop between windows, and processed into 224 mel-scale frequency bins.

\textbf{Data Splitting:} To prevent data leakage from recording conditions, we employed a two-stage splitting strategy. For the Wait\=akere dataset, which contains multiple annotated segments from the same source recordings, we performed file-level splitting: all segments from a given source file were assigned to either the training or test set, never both. Source files were randomly assigned to achieve an approximately 75/25 train/test ratio, though the actual segment-level ratio varies slightly due to differing numbers of annotations per file. For the DOC dataset, which has independent recordings, we performed sample-level splitting but matched the species distribution that resulted from the Wait\=akere file-level split, ensuring comparable class balance across both datasets' train and test sets. This approach balances the need to prevent temporal and acoustic leakage in the Wait\=akere data while maintaining distributional similarity between the two datasets.

\subsection{Normalization Strategies}

We compare six spectrogram normalization approaches to assess their cross-domain robustness: \textbf{Log baseline} is the standard log transform of a linear spectrogram. \textbf{Log+median} applies log transform followed by 5-frame median filter along the time axis. \textbf{Log+normalize} begins with log transform, then uses the quietest 10\% of frames per frequency band to estimate background noise. Outliers (values more than 4 standard deviations from the bottom 10\%) are replaced by sampling uniformly from the non-outlier distribution within each band. We then subtract this background estimate from the original. \textbf{Log+normalize+median} applies the 5-frame median filter before background estimation and subtraction. \textbf{PCEN} is per-channel energy normalization ~\cite{Lostanlen2019}. \textbf{Box-Cox} is a standard power transformation for variance stabilization.

\subsection{Domain Adversarial Training}

We evaluate domain adversarial neural networks (DANN)~\cite{ganin2016domain} as a learned approach to domain adaptation. DANN extends standard classification networks with an auxiliary domain classifier trained adversarially via gradient reversal. During backpropagation, the feature extractor learns representations that are both discriminative for species classification and invariant to domain identity. 

Our implementation adds a domain classification head to the RegNetY-008 backbone, taking features from the final convolutional layer. The domain classifier consists of three fully connected layers (512, 256, 1 units) with batch normalization, ReLU activations, and dropout (0.3), predicting binary domain labels (DOC vs Wait\=akere). A gradient reversal layer with $\lambda=0.3$ inverts gradients flowing back to the feature extractor, encouraging domain-invariant representations. The domain classifier is trained jointly with the species classifier using binary cross-entropy with logits loss.

\subsection{Noise Augmentation}

To investigate whether synthetic acoustic variation can improve generalization, we augment training spectrograms with background noise sampled from the FreeSound dataset. During training, noise is always applied to spectrograms (when enabled) by mixing: $S_{\text{aug}} = (1-\alpha) \cdot S_{\text{orig}} + \alpha \cdot N_{\text{scaled}}$, where $S_{\text{orig}}$ is the original spectrogram, $N_{\text{scaled}}$ is a randomly selected noise sample RMS-scaled to match the signal energy, and $\alpha$ is randomly sampled uniformly from $[0, \min(2 \times \text{intensity}, 1.0)]$ (giving expected value equal to the specified intensity parameter).

We systematically vary two augmentation parameters: \textbf{intensity} ($\alpha \in \{0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$) controls signal-to-noise ratio, and \textbf{variety} (1, 10, 100, or 1000 unique noise samples) controls acoustic diversity. Noise samples are preprocessed identically to bird calls. This design allows us to separate the effects of noise strength from noise diversity~\cite{kumar2024}.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training}

We use a BirdClef 2025-pretrained RegNetY-008 CNN and then apply fine-tuning to our 9 species. Training uses AdamW optimizer (learning rate $10^{-4}$ for backbone, $10^{-3}$ for classifier), batch size 16, cosine annealing, early stopping with patience 15, and mixup with $\alpha=0.25$.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Let $D_s$ and $D_t$ denote source (training) and target (test) domains, and let $A(D_s \to D_t)$ denote accuracy when training on $D_s$ and testing on $D_t$. \textbf{Reduction} is the proportional performance loss, controlling for task difficulty:
\begin{equation}
\text{Reduction}(D_t) = \frac{A(D_t \to D_t) - A(D_s \to D_t)}{A(D_t \to D_t)}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Cross-Domain Accuracy} is the absolute performance $A(D_s \to D_t)$. \textbf{Asymmetry Ratio} is the directional bias, computed as the ratio of reduction percentages for the two transfer directions (DOC→Wait\=akere vs Wait\=akere→DOC).

\subsection{Experimental Design}

For every test we train a model on each dataset and then test both on each dataset, comparing within-domain to cross-domain accuracy. We test our six normalization methods (Log, Log+median, Log+normalize, Log+normalize+median, PCEN, Box-Cox), domain adaptation via an added DANN ~\cite{ganin2016domain} (domain adversarial neural network) head, and finally augment with freefield noise at varying intensities and diversity. We use 3 seeds for every run (reporting mean$\pm$std).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Dataset variance matters}

Domain shift in bird call recognition is fundamentally asymmetric. Models trained on homogeneous recordings suffer substantially larger proportional loss when deployed to heterogeneous environments than the reverse. This pattern holds across all methods tested. This suggests a support mismatch where the DOC distribution forms a strict subset of the Wait\=akere acoustic space: homogeneous training provides insufficient coverage of the covariate range present in heterogeneous test data, while heterogeneous training naturally encompasses the simpler homogeneous case. Noise augmentation and domain adaptation reduce absolute performance gaps but cannot eliminate this asymmetry.

\subsection{Explicit Normalization Outperforms Learned Adaptation}

Simple adaptive background subtraction achieves better cross-domain performance than domain adversarial training, despite requiring no learned parameters. With approximately 700 training samples, DANN provides minimal benefit beyond strong preprocessing. We hypothesize this occurs because explicit normalization already removes much domain-specific structure—the domain signal DANN would learn to ignore has already been eliminated, leaving insufficient information for adversarial alignment. Learned invariance also typically requires larger datasets. In this regime, removing domain variation is more effective than learning to ignore it.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Our results have limited replication (3 seeds for key methods, single runs for others), which constrains statistical robustness. They are also specific to nine New Zealand species (although their calls are relatively representative of many common international species) and have only two recording sources. The two datasets differ in multiple dimensions (equipment, geography, recording practices, potential label noise), preventing causal attribution of observed effects to specific factors—controlled experiments.

A larger study would be needed to test generalization to bigger species sets, other regions, and different equipment. Further, systematically varying individual factors are needed for stronger causal claims.

It should also be noted that aggregate accuracy obscures per-class patterns and potential annotation inconsistencies. We infer mechanisms from accuracy patterns without direct feature representation analysis. 

Our study focuses on 9 New Zealand species with approximately 700 total samples, making the dataset fairly small. We also evaluate only the one CNN-based architecture, though testing on an Audio Spectrogram Transformer shows similar results so far. 

Finally, our noise augmentation uses synthetic background noise, where real environmental noise from target domains may provide more effective regularization. 

We did not examine the possibility of doing initial pseudo-labelling on a subset of the target dataset, which has been tried in the past.
