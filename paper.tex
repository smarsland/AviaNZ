\begin{abstract}
Automated bird call recognition systems trained on one recording environment often produce worse results when deployed to others due to differences in background noise and recording equipment, a problem known as domain shift. To study these domain effects, we construct matched datasets with identical species distributions across nine bird species, but different recording sources (DOC: 59\% in-domain accuracy; Wait\=akere: 31\% in-domain accuracy). We fine-tune a pre-trained bird recognition CNN on each dataset and find substantial domain shift in both transfer directions. When normalized by target dataset difficulty, cross-domain models retain only 52--61\% of the target in-domain baseline, with performance loss of 39--48\% depending on transfer direction.

We test different adaptation methods and find adaptive background subtraction via log scaling with median filtering yields the best cross-domain improvements, achieving 37\% accuracy (65\% of baseline) in one transfer direction. This outperforms other normalization methods and domain adversarial training in our low-data regime (${\sim}$700 samples). While moderate noise augmentation provides modest regularization, noise diversity shows minimal impact. These results demonstrate that substantial domain shift persists even with matched species distributions, and that explicit normalization is more effective than learned adaptation when training data is limited.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{bird call recognition, domain shift, asymmetric transfer, cross-domain evaluation, bioacoustic monitoring}

\section{Introduction}



The automated recognition of bird species from acoustic recordings has improved markedly in recent years~\cite{stowell2022computational,2018_priyadarshani,2018_stowell}. Deep neural networks processing spectrograms of sound data are able to differentiate between the calls of many species, forming the basis for analysis tools such as AviaNZ~\cite{2019_marsland}, BirdNet~\cite{kahl2021birdnet}, and Perch~\cite{van2025perch}, amongst many others. 

However, models often struggle in test environments due to differences in recording equipment (and configuration settings for recorders) and background noise~\cite{2016_darras}. This problem is called \textit{domain shift} and remains a major problem in bioacoustics, as well as many other areas~\cite{Venkateswara2020}. It is of particular concern for biodiversity monitoring, where many different types of acoustic recorder may be used, and annotated training data may only come from a subset of these machine types~\cite{2018_priyadarshani}.

In this work we examine the extent and asymmetry of domain shift in bird call recognition, and evaluate strategies for mitigating it. To isolate domain effects from species composition, we construct matched datasets with identical label distributions but different recording sources. We then systematically compare preprocessing normalization, domain adversarial training, and noise augmentation approaches.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Matched Dataset Construction}

We created two datasets with identical species distributions covering nine classes of commonly found New Zealand birds, comprising introduced European passerines (blackbird, \textit{Turdus merula}; chaffinch \textit{Fringilla coelebs}), native and self-introduced passerines (piwakawaka/fantail, \textit{Rhipidura fuliginosa}; riroriro/grey warbler \textit{Gerygone igata}; miromiro/tomtit, \textit{Petroica macrocephala}; silvereye,\textit{Zosterops lateralis}), a parrot (k\=ak\=a, \textit{Nestor meridionalis}), and an owl (ruru/morepork, \textit{Ninox novaeseelandiae}). A final class combined the vocalisations of two oscine species with overlapping dialectic calls (t\=u\=i, \textit{Prosthemadera novaeseelandiae}; korimako/bellbird, \textit{Anthornis melanura}). The DOC dataset contains 766 files (64 minutes total duration), while the Wait\=akere dataset contains 684 files (73 minutes total duration), providing comparable amounts of training data.

The DOC dataset contains of bird call recordings collected between 2010 and 2023 as part of the New Zealand Department of Conservation (DOC) baseline biodiversity monitoring. These come from across the public conservation land of Aotearoa New Zealand, and are collected using the DOC AR4\footnote{\url{electronics@doc.govt.nz}} recorder sampling at 32 kHz. Bird calls in this dataset were manually identified by a combination of spectrogram visualisation and listening to record the species present within one minute of recording, with the calls extracted automatically from these records to make 3-12 second long recordings. The dataset is available at \url{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ollypowell/new-zealand-bird-sound} and has an Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license. We subsequently processed the data to correct some labels and ensure that the labelled species was clearly present. 

Our second dataset comes from recordings from six sites in the Wait\=akere ranges, an area of national park approximately 25 km from the city of Auckland in Aotearoa New Zealand. The data were collected as part of a MSc project~\cite{JoeMsc2024} using a combination of the Frontier Labs Bar- LT\footnote{\url{https://www.frontierlabs.com.au/bar-lt}} and the DOC AR4, both recording at 32 kHz. For both datasets, spectrograms were extracted in AviaNZ using 25 ms Hann window with a 10 ms hop between windows, and processed into 224 mel-scale frequency bins.

\textbf{Data Splitting:} To prevent data leakage from recording conditions, we employed a two-stage splitting strategy. For the Wait\=akere dataset, which contains multiple annotated segments from the same source recordings, we performed file-level splitting: all segments from a given source file were assigned to either the training or test set, never both. Source files were randomly assigned to achieve an approximately 75/25 train/test ratio, though the actual segment-level ratio varies slightly due to differing numbers of annotations per file. For the DOC dataset, which has independent recordings, we performed sample-level splitting but matched the species distribution that resulted from the Wait\=akere file-level split, ensuring comparable class balance across the training and testing sets of both datasets. This approach balances the need to prevent temporal and acoustic leakage in the Wait\=akere data while maintaining distributional similarity between the two datasets.

\subsection{Normalization Strategies}

We compare six spectrogram normalization approaches to assess their cross-domain robustness: \textbf{Log baseline} is the standard log transform of a linear spectrogram. \textbf{Log+median} applies log transform followed by 5-frame median filter along the time axis. \textbf{Log+normalize} begins with log transform, then uses the quietest 10\% of frames per frequency band to estimate background noise. Outliers (values more than 4 standard deviations from the bottom 10\%) are replaced by sampling randomly from the non-outlier distribution within each band. We then subtract this background estimate from the original. \textbf{Log+normalize+median} applies the 5-frame median filter before background estimation and subtraction. \textbf{PCEN} is per-channel energy normalization~\cite{Lostanlen2019}. \textbf{Box-Cox} is a standard power transformation for variance stabilization \cite{1964_box_cox}.

\subsection{Domain Adversarial Training}

We evaluate domain adversarial neural networks (DANN)~\cite{ganin2016domain} as a learned approach to domain adaptation. DANN extends standard classification networks with an auxiliary domain classifier trained adversarially via gradient reversal. During training the feature extractor learns representations that are both discriminative for species classification and invariant to domain identity. 

Our implementation adds a domain classification head to the RegNetY-008 backbone, taking features from the final convolutional layer. The domain classifier consists of three fully connected layers (running from 512 units to 256 to 1) with batch normalization, ReLU activations, and dropout (0.3), predicting binary domain labels (DOC vs Wait\=akere). A gradient reversal layer with $\lambda=0.3$ inverts gradients flowing back to the feature extractor, encouraging domain-invariant representations. The domain classifier is trained jointly with the species classifier using binary cross-entropy with logits loss.

\subsection{Noise Augmentation}

To investigate whether synthetic acoustic variation can improve generalization, we augment training spectrograms with background noise sampled from the FreeSound dataset\footnote{\url{http://freesound.org}}. During training, noise is always applied to spectrograms (when enabled) by mixing: $S_{\text{aug}} = (1-\alpha) \cdot S_{\text{orig}} + \alpha \cdot N_{\text{scaled}}$, where $S_{\text{orig}}$ is the original spectrogram, $N_{\text{scaled}}$ is a randomly selected noise sample RMS-scaled to match the signal energy, and $\alpha$ is randomly sampled uniformly from $[0, \min(2 \times \text{intensity}, 1.0)]$ (giving expected value equal to the specified intensity parameter).

We systematically vary two augmentation parameters: \textbf{intensity} ($\alpha \in \{0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$) controls signal-to-noise ratio, and \textbf{variety} (1, 10, 100, or 1000 unique noise samples) controls acoustic diversity. Noise samples are preprocessed identically to bird calls. This design allows us to separate the effects of noise strength from noise diversity~\cite{kumar2024}.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training}

We use a BirdClef 2025-pretrained RegNetY-008 CNN \cite{shionao7_regnrty008_2024} and then apply fine-tuning to our nine species. Training uses the AdamW optimizer (learning rate $10^{-4}$ for backbone, $10^{-3}$ for classifier), batch size 16, cosine annealing, early stopping with patience 15, and mixup with $\alpha=0.25$.

\textbf{Data Augmentation:} During training, we apply standard spectrogram augmentations to improve generalization: time stretching (±10\%, 50\% probability), frequency shifting (±10 mel bins, 50\% probability), temporal rolling (random circular shift along time axis), and SpecAugment-style time/frequency masking (2 rounds per sample, masking up to 20\% of time and 48 frequency bins). These augmentations are disabled during evaluation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Let $D_s$ and $D_t$ denote source (training) and target (test) domains, and let $A(D_s \to D_t)$ denote exact accuracy (all classes) when training on $D_s$ and testing on $D_t$. \textbf{Reduction} is the proportional performance loss relative to the target domain's in-domain baseline, controlling for target dataset difficulty:
\begin{equation}
\text{Reduction}(D_s \to D_t) = \frac{A(D_t \to D_t) - A(D_s \to D_t)}{A(D_t \to D_t)}
\end{equation}
This measures how much worse a model trained on $D_s$ performs on $D_t$ compared to a model trained directly on $D_t$. \textbf{Cross-Domain Accuracy} is the absolute performance $A(D_s \to D_t)$. \textbf{Asymmetry Ratio} is the directional bias, computed as the ratio of reduction percentages for the two transfer directions (DOC→Wait\=akere vs Wait\=akere→DOC).

\subsection{Experimental Design}

For every test we train a model on the training data from one dataset and then test it on the test data of both datasets, comparing within-domain to cross-domain accuracy. We test our six normalization methods (Log, Log+median, Log+normalize, Log+normalize+median, PCEN, Box-Cox), domain adaptation via an added DANN (domain adversarial neural network)~\cite{ganin2016domain}  head, and finally augment with freefield noise at varying intensities and diversity. We use 3 seeds for every run (reporting mean$\pm$std).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Substantial Domain Shift Persists}

Domain shift in bird call recognition remains substantial even with matched species distributions. Using the Log baseline, cross-domain models achieve only 52--61\% of the target in-domain baseline (47.6\% and 38.6\% reduction for Wait\=akere→DOC and DOC→Wait\=akere respectively). Crucially, these reductions are measured relative to each target dataset's intrinsic difficulty---DOC has a 59\% in-domain baseline while Wait\=akere has only 31\%. Although Wait\=akere→DOC achieves higher absolute cross-domain accuracy (31\% vs 19\%), it retains only 52\% of the target baseline compared to 61\% for the reverse direction, demonstrating that absolute accuracy can be misleading without normalizing for dataset difficulty.

The two datasets differ along multiple dimensions (recording equipment, geography, recording practices, annotation protocols, and intrinsic task difficulty), making it impossible to attribute the observed asymmetry to any single factor. What is clear is that substantial performance loss occurs in both transfer directions, and that this loss cannot be eliminated through the adaptation methods we tested, though preprocessing normalization provides meaningful improvements.

\subsection{Explicit Normalization Outperforms Learned Adaptation}

Adaptive background subtraction (Log+normalize+median) achieves better cross-domain performance than domain adversarial training. With approximately 700 training samples, DANN provides no benefit beyond strong preprocessing. We hypothesize this occurs because explicit normalization already removes much domain-specific structure—the domain signal DANN would learn to ignore has already been eliminated, leaving insufficient information for adversarial alignment. Learned invariance also typically requires larger datasets. In this low-data regime, removing domain variation by normalization is more effective than learning to ignore it. The negative reduction percentages observed for some methods (indicating cross-domain performance exceeding target in-domain baseline) suggest that preprocessing can sometimes over-correct, creating artifacts that harm in-domain performance while inadvertently benefiting cross-domain transfer.

\subsection{Dataset Independence and Generalization Gaps}

An unexpected finding concerns the validation-to-test performance gap, which differs substantially between datasets. For Wait\=akere (file-level split), models achieve 60--62\% validation accuracy but drop to 31--35\% on the test set, a gap of 26--30 percentage points. For DOC (sample-level split), validation accuracy of 71--73\% drops to 57--59\% at test time, only a 12--16 percentage point gap. The Wait\=akere generalization gap is approximately twice as large as DOC's.

This asymmetry suggests the DOC dataset may have less independence than expected. Despite samples coming from nationwide recordings across diverse locations, the smaller generalization gap implies either: (1) less acoustic diversity than anticipated, with similar recording conditions producing relatively homogeneous data, or (2) possible subtle data leakage, though this seems less likely given the geographic spread. The Wait\=akere file-level split, which ensures all segments from the same source recording are assigned to train or test (never both), produces the expected larger gap for truly independent test data. This finding highlights that geographic diversity does not automatically guarantee acoustic diversity, and raises questions about what constitutes truly independent evaluation data in bioacoustic monitoring.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Our results have limited replication (five repetitions for most experiments, three for some), which constrains statistical robustness. They are also specific to nine New Zealand species (although their calls are relatively representative of many common international species) and have only two recording sources. The two datasets differ in multiple dimensions (equipment, geography, recording practices, potential label noise), preventing causal attribution of observed effects to specific factors—controlled experiments.

A larger study would be needed to test generalization to bigger species sets, other regions, and different equipment. Further, systematically varying individual factors are needed for stronger causal claims.

It should also be noted that aggregate accuracy obscures per-class patterns and potential annotation inconsistencies. We infer mechanisms from accuracy patterns without direct feature representation analysis. 

Our study focuses on 9 New Zealand species with approximately 700 total samples, making the dataset fairly small. We also evaluate only the one CNN-based architecture, though testing on an Audio Spectrogram Transformer shows similar results so far. 

Finally, our noise augmentation uses synthetic background noise, where real environmental noise from target domains may provide more effective regularization. 

We did not examine the possibility of doing initial pseudo-labelling on a subset of the target dataset, which has been tried in the past.
