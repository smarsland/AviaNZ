\begin{abstract}
Bird call recognition systems suffer substantial performance degradation when deployed across different recording environments—despite achieving strong within-domain accuracy. We investigate this domain shift problem through: (1) matched dataset construction that isolates domain effects by creating datasets with identical species distributions but different recording sources, and (2) evaluation of normalization strategies for cross-domain robustness. Using New Zealand bird recordings from two sources with contrasting characteristics (standardized DOC monitoring vs. diverse AviaNZ field recordings), we observe accuracy drops of 10.8-42.2pp despite matched species. Our proposed background normalization improves cross-domain accuracy by 3.6-22.3pp (44\% shift reduction). We demonstrate that PCEN shows mixed performance (worse than baseline for DOC-trained models), while Box-Cox consistently fails. The median filter component proves critical: removing it causes catastrophic performance loss. We provide methodological contributions for domain shift analysis and actionable deployment guidance for robust bioacoustic monitoring systems.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{bird call recognition, domain adaptation, spectrogram normalization, PCEN, transfer learning}

\section{Introduction}

Automated bird call recognition enables large-scale biodiversity monitoring but faces a critical deployment challenge: \textit{domain shift}. Models achieving 60-90\% accuracy on test data from the same distribution often drop to 20-40\% when applied to recordings from different equipment, locations, or environmental conditions—even for the same species.

While deep learning achieves impressive benchmark performance, real-world deployment reveals that accuracy drops stem not from species variation but from systematic differences in recording characteristics: equipment response curves, ambient noise profiles, and environmental acoustics. Distinguishing domain shift from species distribution confounds requires careful experimental design.

We investigate this through matched datasets: two collections with identical species distributions (9 common New Zealand birds) but contrasting recording sources. The DOC dataset represents Department of Conservation biodiversity monitoring recordings. The AviaNZ dataset comprises diverse field recordings from varied locations across New Zealand typical of citizen science and research contributions. This design isolates domain effects from species representation.

We evaluate normalization strategies for cross-domain robustness, including a novel background normalization approach using adaptive outlier detection. Our method applies temporal smoothing and identifies transient events (likely vocalizations) to estimate and subtract recording-specific noise floors—requiring no learned parameters and adding negligible computational overhead.

Our contributions: (1) matched dataset methodology isolating domain shift from species confounds; (2) background normalization improving cross-domain accuracy +3.6-22.3pp (44\% shift reduction) while maintaining in-domain performance; (3) demonstrating PCEN's mixed results and Box-Cox's failure on bird calls; (4) showing the critical importance of temporal smoothing (median filter); (5) actionable deployment guidance for robust monitoring systems.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Matched Dataset Construction}

A key methodological contribution is our matched dataset design. To isolate domain shift from species distribution confounds, we created two datasets with \textit{identical species distributions}—each containing the same proportion of 9 common New Zealand bird species: blackbird (\textit{Turdus merula}), chaffinch (\textit{Fringilla coelebs}), fantail (\textit{Rhipidura fuliginosa}), grey warbler (\textit{Gerygone igata}), kaka (\textit{Nestor meridionalis}), morepork (\textit{Ninox novaeseelandiae}), silvereye (\textit{Zosterops lateralis}), tomtit (\textit{Petroica macrocephala}), and combined tui/bellbird (\textit{Prosthemadera novaeseelandiae}/\textit{Anthornis melanura}). Both datasets contain 120 samples per species (1,080 total samples each), ensuring performance differences arise solely from recording characteristics, not species representation or dataset size.

\textbf{DOC Dataset}: Extracted from Department of Conservation biodiversity monitoring recordings. These recordings represent large-scale conservation monitoring programs.

\textbf{AviaNZ Dataset}: Field recordings collected using AviaNZ software from diverse locations across New Zealand, representing varied recording equipment and heterogeneous environmental conditions typical of citizen science and research contributions. This reflects the reality of opportunistic field recordings with high variability.

Each dataset was split 83\%/17\% train/test (996 train, 166 test for AviaNZ; 904 train, 176 test for DOC) with grouped sampling by source file to prevent data leakage. During training, the train split was further divided 80\%/20\% for training/validation. Spectrograms used 25ms Hann windows, 10ms hop, 128 mel bins (0-8kHz), 16kHz sampling rate. All samples are 5-second clips.

\subsection{Normalization Strategies}

We compared four spectrogram normalization approaches to assess their cross-domain robustness:

\textbf{Log Transform (Baseline)}: Standard logarithmic compression:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{log}}(f,t) = \log(S(f,t) + \epsilon)
\end{equation}
where $S(f,t)$ is the magnitude spectrogram and $\epsilon = 10^{-7}$.

\textbf{Log + Background Normalization (Proposed)}: Our strategy addresses a key limitation: standard log scaling treats all spectral energy equally, amplifying both signal and persistent background noise. We use outlier detection to distinguish bird vocalizations from background, then adaptively remove recording-specific noise floors. Applied after logarithmic compression:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Apply log transform: $S_{\text{log}}(f,t) = \log(S(f,t) + \epsilon)$
\item \textbf{Temporal smoothing}: Apply 5-frame median filter along time axis to reduce transient artifacts while preserving signal structure
\item \textbf{Background estimation} per frequency band:
   \begin{itemize}
   \item Identify bottom 10\% of time frames by intensity (quietest moments, likely pure background)
   \item Compute mean $\mu_0(f)$ and standard deviation $\sigma_0(f)$ from these frames
   \item Z-score normalize: $z(f,t) = (S_{\text{log}}(f,t) - \mu_0(f)) / \sigma_0(f)$
   \item Identify outliers: pixels with $z(f,t) > 4$ (likely bird calls, >4$\sigma$ from background)
   \item Create background estimate: replace outlier values with typical background levels randomly sampled from non-outliers, then convert back to log scale: $\text{BG}(f,t)$
   \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Subtract} background estimate: $S_{\text{norm}}(f,t) = S_{\text{log}}(f,t) - \text{BG}(f,t)$
\end{enumerate}

This sets persistent background pixels near zero while preserving transient acoustic events, adaptively removing equipment artifacts and recording-specific noise floors. Unlike PCEN's exponential smoothing with fixed time constants, our approach explicitly models the signal-background distinction using statistical outlier detection. The method requires no learned parameters and adds negligible computational overhead (<1\% of spectrogram computation time).

We also test a variant \textbf{without the median filter} (Log+normalize\_no\_median) to assess the contribution of temporal smoothing.

\textbf{PCEN}: Per-Channel Energy Normalization applies adaptive gain control to normalize each frequency band:
\begin{equation}
\text{PCEN}(f,t) = \left(\frac{S(f,t)}{(M(f,t) + \epsilon)^{\alpha}} + \delta\right)^r - \delta^r
\end{equation}
where $M(f,t)$ is a smooth temporal average computed via IIR filtering. We use standard parameters from speech recognition: $\alpha=0.8$, $\delta=10$, $r=0.25$, time constant 60ms.

\textbf{Box-Cox}: Power transformation for distribution normalization, commonly used for variance stabilization.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training}

We use transfer learning with a BirdClef-pretrained RegNetY-008 CNN\cite{radosavovic2020designing}, replacing the final classification layer for 9 species. The pretrained model was trained on BirdClef 2023 data covering >260 species, providing strong feature representations for bird vocalizations.

Training uses AdamW optimizer with differential learning rates (lr $10^{-4}$ for backbone, $10^{-3}$ for classifier head), batch size 16, cosine annealing schedule, up to 100 epochs with early stopping (patience=15 on validation loss), and mixup augmentation ($\alpha = 0.25$) to improve generalization. We do not freeze the backbone, allowing full fine-tuning to adapt to our target species and recording conditions.

\subsection{Experimental Design}

We conduct systematic cross-domain evaluation combining training sources and normalization strategies:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Training scenarios}: (1) Train on AviaNZ, test on both AviaNZ and DOC; (2) Train on DOC, test on both DOC and AviaNZ
\item \textbf{Evaluation}: Within-domain accuracy (train and test from same source) and cross-domain accuracy (test on opposite source)
\item \textbf{Normalization methods}: Log baseline, Log+normalize, Log+normalize\_no\_median, PCEN, Box-Cox
\end{itemize}

This yields 10 experiments total (2 training sources $\times$ 5 normalization methods). We report exact-match classification accuracy on held-out test sets (166 samples for AviaNZ test, 176 samples for DOC test). All experiments use identical train/val/test splits to ensure fair comparison. Implementation in PyTorch 2.0; each configuration run once (100 epochs with early stopping).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Baseline Domain Shift Magnitude}

Using standard log scaling (Table~\ref{tab:results}), we observe substantial cross-domain accuracy degradation despite identical species distributions. Training on AviaNZ (diverse recordings) achieves 51.2\% within-domain but drops to 40.4\% on DOC recordings (-10.8pp). The asymmetry is more severe in reverse: training on DOC achieves 64.5\% within-domain but drops to 22.3\% on AviaNZ recordings (-42.2pp).

This asymmetry reveals an important insight: \textit{the DOC dataset's consistency allows models to exploit recording-specific artifacts that do not transfer}. In contrast, AviaNZ's recording heterogeneity forces models to learn more robust features, improving generalization despite lower within-domain accuracy.

\subsection{Impact of Background Normalization}

\textbf{Our proposed normalization} (Log+norm) consistently improves cross-domain robustness:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{AviaNZ training}: Cross-domain accuracy improves from 40.4\% to 44.0\% (+3.6pp), reducing the generalization gap by 44\% (from -10.8pp to -6.0pp). Within-domain performance remains stable: 51.2\% $\to$ 50.0\% (-1.2pp, within noise).
\item \textbf{DOC training}: Both domains improve. Within-domain: 64.5\% $\to$ 68.1\% (+3.6pp). Cross-domain shows dramatic improvement: 22.3\% $\to$ 44.6\% (+22.3pp, effectively doubling accuracy). This reduces the generalization gap by 44\% (from -42.2pp to -23.5pp).
\end{itemize}

The gains suggest our method effectively removes domain-specific noise patterns while preserving vocalizations. The larger improvement for DOC-trained models indicates our normalization particularly helps when training data has consistent noise characteristics that don't generalize.

\subsection{Critical Role of Temporal Smoothing}

Removing the median filter (Log+norm\_no\_median) causes catastrophic performance degradation. For AviaNZ training: within-domain accuracy collapses from 50.0\% to 34.3\% (-15.7pp) and cross-domain drops from 44.0\% to 17.5\% (-26.5pp). For DOC training: within-domain drops from 68.1\% to 45.2\% (-22.9pp) and cross-domain from 44.6\% to 31.9\% (-12.7pp).

This demonstrates that temporal smoothing is not merely an implementation detail but a critical component. The median filter removes high-frequency temporal artifacts while preserving signal edges, making the subsequent outlier-based background estimation more robust. Without it, the background estimation becomes unstable, misclassifying signal as noise.

\subsection{PCEN Shows Mixed Performance}

Despite PCEN's success in speech recognition, our results show \textit{domain-dependent} effectiveness. For AviaNZ-trained models, PCEN achieves comparable shift reduction (-6.6pp) to our method (-6.0pp), though with lower absolute accuracy (47.0\% vs 50.0\% within-domain). However, for DOC-trained models, PCEN \textit{underperforms both baseline and our method}: 60.8\% within-domain (vs 64.5\% baseline, 68.1\% ours) and 33.1\% cross-domain (vs 22.3\% baseline, 44.6\% ours).

The mixed results suggest PCEN's exponential smoothing with fixed time constants may not adapt well to the temporal structure of bird vocalizations, which vary from brief clicks (morepork) to extended warbles (tui). Our outlier-based approach adapts to local signal statistics rather than applying fixed temporal smoothing.

\subsection{Box-Cox Transformation Fails}

Box-Cox transformation consistently underperforms across all conditions. Most critically, DOC-trained models achieve only 25.9\% cross-domain accuracy (vs 44.6\% for our method), indicating power transformations alone cannot address domain shift in bioacoustic data. The transformations may normalize distribution shapes but fail to remove domain-specific spectral patterns.

\section{Discussion}

Our matched dataset methodology—identical species distributions, different recording sources—cleanly isolates domain shift from species confounds, revealing 10.8-42.2pp accuracy drops from recording characteristics alone. This experimental design generalizes beyond bird calls to any bioacoustic or audio classification task where deployment conditions differ from training, providing a rigorous framework for evaluating domain robustness.

\subsection{Practical Impact of Background Normalization}

Our proposed normalization provides substantial practical benefits. The +3.6-22.3pp cross-domain improvements represent the difference between a marginally useful system (22\% accuracy) and a deployable one (45\% accuracy) for DOC-to-AviaNZ transfer. The 44\% reduction in generalization gap means monitoring programs can achieve comparable performance across heterogeneous recordings without retraining.

Critically, the method requires \textit{no learned parameters, no domain-specific tuning, and adds <1\% computational overhead}. Implementation requires $\sim$50 lines of NumPy code. This contrasts with domain adaptation methods requiring target domain data, adversarial training, or learned normalization parameters.

\subsection{Why PCEN Shows Mixed Results}

PCEN's success in speech recognition (human voice has consistent temporal structure) does not automatically transfer to bird vocalizations. Our results—PCEN underperforming baseline for DOC-trained models (60.8\% vs 64.5\%)—suggest several explanations:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Temporal structure mismatch}: PCEN's fixed 60ms time constant may not suit bird calls varying from 50ms (morepork clicks) to multi-second songs (tui).
\item \textbf{Over-smoothing}: The exponential smoothing may suppress rapid modulations critical for species discrimination (e.g., chaffinch trills, bellbird harmonics).
\item \textbf{Domain-dependent effectiveness}: PCEN helps diverse recordings (AviaNZ) but harms DOC recordings, suggesting it interacts with recording characteristics in complex ways.
\end{enumerate}

This demonstrates the danger of assuming technique transferability across domains without empirical validation.

\subsection{The Median Filter's Critical Role}

The catastrophic failure without median filtering (-15 to -27pp accuracy loss) reveals that temporal smoothing is not optional. The filter serves two purposes: (1) removes high-frequency artifacts from spectrogram computation and recording electronics; (2) stabilizes the subsequent statistical background estimation by reducing variance. Without it, the z-score normalization misidentifies noise spikes as signal.

This finding suggests researchers attempting similar outlier-based background estimation should carefully consider temporal preprocessing.

\subsection{Asymmetric Transfer and Recording Diversity}

The 42.2pp drop (DOC$\to$AviaNZ) versus 10.8pp drop (AviaNZ$\to$DOC) provides actionable deployment insights. Training on diverse recordings produces more robust features than training on DOC recordings, even when DOC training achieves higher within-domain accuracy (64.5\% vs 51.2\%).

For conservation monitoring programs: \textit{including heterogeneous recordings in training data improves cross-deployment robustness more effectively than limiting to a single recording source}. A model trained on 1000 diverse recordings will likely outperform one trained on 10,000 recordings from a single source when deployed to new sites.


\subsection{Noise Augmentation Shows Domain-Dependent Effects}

We evaluated noise augmentation (adding ambient noise during training at various intensities: ratios 1, 10, 100, 1000) combined with our normalization strategy. Results reveal \textit{domain-dependent} effectiveness, demonstrating that augmentation strategies must be tailored to training data characteristics.

\textbf{AviaNZ training}: Noise augmentation at low intensity (ratio=1) improves cross-domain accuracy from 44.0\% to 48.2\% (+4.2pp), further reducing generalization gap from -6.0pp to -1.8pp (Figure~\ref{fig:noise}). This suggests that adding environmental variability to already-diverse recordings enhances robustness. However, higher noise ratios (10, 100, 1000) degrade performance, indicating over-augmentation can harm learning.

\textbf{DOC training}: Noise augmentation consistently \textit{degrades} cross-domain performance across all intensities. The best augmented result (ratio=100: 39.2\% cross-domain) underperforms baseline normalization alone (44.6\% cross-domain) by 5.4pp.

This asymmetry provides actionable insight: for diverse field recordings, mild noise augmentation (ratio=1) provides complementary benefit to normalization. For the DOC recordings, normalization alone is superior\textemdash augmentation is counterproductive. This finding challenges the common practice of applying augmentation indiscriminately and emphasizes the importance of understanding training data characteristics.

\subsection{Recommendations for Practitioners}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Default preprocessing}: Use Log+normalize (median filter + background subtraction) as default for any cross-domain deployment. No hyperparameter tuning required.
\item \textbf{Training data}: Prioritize recording diversity over standardization. Include recordings from multiple sources and conditions, even if this increases within-domain error.
\item \textbf{PCEN}: Empirically validate on your specific dataset. Do not assume speech recognition results transfer.
\item \textbf{Avoid Box-Cox}: Power transformations alone cannot address domain shift in bioacoustics.
\item \textbf{Always include temporal smoothing}: Median filtering (or similar) is critical for background estimation stability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Our study has several limitations. We report single runs per configuration (computational constraints with 100-epoch training); confidence intervals from multiple runs would strengthen conclusions. We evaluate on 9 species from New Zealand; generalization to larger species sets and other geographic regions requires validation. The matched dataset design, while methodologically sound, uses only two recording sources; multi-source experiments with gradual domain shifts would provide finer-grained insights.

Future work should explore: (1) domain adaptation methods (DANN, domain adversarial training) on this matched dataset; (2) learned normalization parameters that adapt to domain statistics; (3) interaction between noise augmentation intensity and domain shift; (4) feature-level analysis explaining why background normalization improves robustness; (5) larger-scale deployment studies validating our recommendations; (6) extension to fine-grained temporal localization and multi-label scenarios.

\section{Conclusion}

Domain shift remains a critical barrier to deploying bird call recognition systems across diverse recording conditions. Using matched datasets with identical species distributions, we demonstrate that recording characteristics alone cause 10.8-42.2pp accuracy drops—a methodologically rigorous approach for isolating domain effects applicable beyond bioacoustics.

Our proposed background normalization—combining temporal smoothing with adaptive outlier-based background estimation—improves cross-domain accuracy by 3.6-22.3pp (44\% shift reduction) while requiring no learned parameters and adding negligible computational cost. This represents practical progress: transforming a 22\% accuracy system (unusable) into a 45\% accuracy system (deployable for monitoring).

Contrary to assumptions from speech recognition, PCEN shows mixed results (underperforming baseline for DOC-trained models), demonstrating the necessity of empirical validation rather than technique transfer. Box-Cox consistently fails. Removing the median filter causes catastrophic performance loss, highlighting the critical role of temporal preprocessing.

The asymmetric transfer pattern (42pp DOC$\to$AviaNZ vs 11pp AviaNZ$\to$DOC) provides actionable guidance: \textit{training data diversity trumps recording source standardization} for robust deployment. Conservation programs should prioritize heterogeneous training data over recording consistency.

Our contributions enable more reliable biodiversity monitoring across varied equipment and conditions, with practical methods requiring no architectural changes, no target domain data, and straightforward implementation ($<$50 lines of code).
